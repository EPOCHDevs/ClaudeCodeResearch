Title: 3m Rolling Correlation of BDXY to US Market Variables (2024-2026)
Source: BMO FX macro report 3-9-26.pdf (page 2)
Date: 2026-03-09
Type: chart

Insight: The USD is re-establishing its correlation with 2-year and 10-year rate differentials while its relationship with yield curve shape has broken down, signaling a shift in FX dynamics toward rate-driven moves rather than traditional steepening-induced weakness.

Context (above):
There’s more to the move than simple risk-on/risk-off. Recent price action across global markets reflects an  
intersection of positioning and short-term valuation, the prevailing narrative, and the latest macro shock. FX moves,  
in particular, are not just about risk aversion or crowded positioning. A more important theme is that the US macro  
backdrop has improved—both absolutely and relative to the rest of the world. Our high-frequency growth and inflation  
factors place the USD among the strongest currencies across the G10 and EM universe. As shown in the first chart, the  
USD sits firmly in the top-right quadrant—the most constructive macro position—alongside a small group of currencies  
including MXN, AUD, BRL, and KRW. By contrast, EUR, GBP, CHF, and JPY sit in the lower half of the distribution,  
reflecting a deceleration in inflation momentum. While growth remains positive for several of these economies, the 
USD growth signal sits above both the G10 and EM aggregates and is still accelerating. This configuration suggests  
the US macro story is stronger than what is currently priced, while Europe’s outlook looks increasingly fragile. Consistent  
with this shift, the USD’s correlation structure is changing. As shown in the second chart, the dollar is re-engaging  
with 2y and 10y rate differentials, while its relationship with the shape of the yield curve has effectively broken down.  
In other words, the familiar “weaker USD, steeper curve” dynamic is off the table for now.

Context (below):
Safe havens aren’t equal. One extension of recent market themes is that some traditional havens are no longer doing  
their job. Our risk-sentiment index (MOOD) has flipped decisively into risk-off territory for the first time in nearly  
a year, with the speed of the move notable. The main drivers have been the credit and volatility components; equities  
and fixed income had been dragging the index lower earlier this year, but not enough to trigger a full risk-off signal. Our  
global macro framework (MAPS) treats this as a growth shock, with uneven implications across countries and currencies.  
That’s also showing up in shifting FX relationships to MOOD. A spike in risk-off is now broadly bullish for the USD and  
gold—a clear change from last year. By contrast, CHF and JPY, while still havens, are behaving more like pro-cyclical  
currencies, falling as MOOD deteriorates. For JPY, this likely reflects the energy-driven nature of the shock and a negative  
terms of trade impulse; for CHF, rising SNB discomfort with currency strength and renewed intervention risk are key. The  
broader message is clear: when MOOD sours, the USD strengthens, increasingly versus TWD, ZAR, BRL, AUD, MXN, HUF,  
PLN, and EUR. We continue to prefer USD longs against low-yielding G10 currencies such as EUR, CHF, GBP, and SEK.
