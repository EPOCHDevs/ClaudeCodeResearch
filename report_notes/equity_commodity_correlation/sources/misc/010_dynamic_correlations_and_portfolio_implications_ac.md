<div><div><section><h2>1. Introduction</h2><p><span><span>Today, the world <a href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/agricultural-and-biological-sciences/face">faces</a> a new pandemic identified as Novel </span><a href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/agricultural-and-biological-sciences/coronavirinae">Coronavirus</a> (COVID-19), which has restructured global financial markets (</span><a href="#bib15"><span><span>Daube, 2020</span></span></a>). Particularly, the S&amp;P 500 and Nasdaq 100 index saw 14.9% and 12.4% declines in their market value from March 6, 2020, to March 18, 2020, respectively (<a href="#bib47"><span><span>Shehzad et al., 2020a</span></span></a>).<a href="#fn1"><span><span><sup>1</sup></span></span></a><a href="#bib48"><span><span>Shehzad et al. (2020b)</span></span></a> reveal that the COVID-19 crisis has been more harmful than the global financial crisis (GFC) of 2007–2009. However, commodity futures have different return and volatility patterns from stock markets and diversify financial risk (<a href="#bib27"><span><span>Kang et al., 2017</span></span></a><span>). Accordingly, <a href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/economics-econometrics-and-finance/financial-institution">financial institutions</a> and investors invest large amounts in commodity futures, leading to the emergence of these markets as a valued asset class. Hence, a suitable portfolio can be found for US investors through commodity futures during the COVID-19 era. For this purpose, the study selects S&amp;P 500, wheat futures, beverages futures, West Intermediate Texas (WTI) oil futures, Brent oil futures, and gold futures. </span><a href="#bib6"><span><span>Batten et al. (2017)</span></span></a> reveal the importance of oil markets for diversifying financial risk. Moreover, <a href="#bib34"><span><span>Mensi et al. (2013)</span></span></a> show that commodities, i.e., wheat, beverages, crude oil, and gold, improve investors' returns during a regular period. <a href="#bib44"><span><span>Sadorsky (2012)</span></span></a> argues that oil markets offer discounted hedges for emerging markets. Moreover, <a href="#bib7"><span><span>Baur and McDermott (2010)</span></span></a><span> state that gold not only possesses hedging properties but also acts as a <a href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/economics-econometrics-and-finance/safe-haven">safe haven</a> for US and European stock markets; though these findings are not confirmed for BRICS, Australian, or Canadian equities. </span><a href="#bib42"><span><span>Reboredo (2013)</span></span></a> reveals that gold acts as a safe haven and hedge against US dollar price movements. <a href="#bib18"><span><span>El Hedi Arouri et al. (2015)</span></span></a><span> show that gold is a vital instrument against Chinese stock to reduce financial risk. Therefore, revealing the hedge properties, time-varying correlations, <a href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/economics-econometrics-and-finance/spillover-effect">spillover</a> impacts, and downside risks of these markets becomes essential during the normal period and COVID-19 era. The most corroborated approach to measuring the downside risk of financial markets is the value at risk (VaR) method established by </span><a href="#bib25"><span><span>Jorion (1996)</span></span></a>. However, <a href="#bib13"><span><span>Cheng et al. (2004)</span></span></a> argue about its inefficiency owing to its convexity; nonetheless, no authenticated instrument can quantify the downside risk of financial markets except VaR (<a href="#bib54"><span><span>Sampid and Hasim, 2018</span></span></a>). The correct estimation of VaR is substantially dependent on the nature of the volatility model. An accurate volatility model not only deals with the fat-tailed properties of the distribution but also with outsized financial instruments (<a href="#bib33"><span><span>Malz, 2011</span></span></a><span>). To specify the VaR of financial markets, a multivariate approach to the generalized autoregressive conditional <a href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/agricultural-and-biological-sciences/heteroskedasticity">heteroskedasticity</a><span> (GARCH) model can be taken. Numerous multivariate GARCH approaches have been established to compute the covariance between financial markets, such as CCC-GARCH, DCC-GARCH, GO-GARCH, and BEKK-GARCH models. This study implements the dynamic conditional correlation (DCC) multivariate exponential <a href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/economics-econometrics-and-finance/generalized-autoregressive-conditional-heteroskedasticity">generalized autoregressive conditional heteroskedasticity</a> (MEGARCH) approach established by </span></span><a href="#bib19"><span><span>Engle (2002)</span></span></a> and <a href="#bib39"><span><span>Nelson (1991)</span></span></a>. The investigation employs the vector autoregressive (VAR) approach to capture the effect of returns transmission among the commodity futures and stock markets. It applies the student-t distribution to model the fat-tailed properties of the returns distribution. The DCC model is capable of dynamically handling the time-varying correlation between financial assets and many matrices (<a href="#bib2"><span><span>Ahmad et al., 2013</span></span></a>). In addition, the MEGARCH approach correctly calculates the leverage impact (<a href="#bib50"><span><span>Sikhosana and Aye, 2018</span></span></a>).</p><p>Various studies ascertain the returns transmission and volatility spillover effects between financial markets, gold, oil, and other commodities for the standard period, e.g., <a href="#bib12"><span><span>Chan et al. (2011)</span></span></a>, and <a href="#bib4"><span><span>Aruga and Managi (2011)</span></span></a>. However, past research does not consider the optimal portfolio, hedging, or portfolio VaR of these markets during the COVID-19 era. Furthermore, no study compares the behavior of these markets before and during the first wave of the COVID-19period.<a href="#fn2"><span><span><sup>2</sup></span></span></a><span> The contribution of this analysis is evaluating and comparing investment strategies during the normal and COVID-19 eras. The research studies the bidirectional and unidirectional returns and volatility transmission effects between the stock market and commodity futures to conclude which markets are the net receivers and transmitters of financial risk. The examination scrutinizes portfolio VaR through normal and student-t density at 95% and 99% confidence levels. The research assesses the power of DCC-MEGARCH to estimate the exact VaR for a portfolio of S&amp;P 500 and commodity futures. The author believes that analyzing these markets before and during the COVID-19 period brings essential facts in the field of <a href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/economics-econometrics-and-finance/finance">finance</a>. The essential research queries of this project are, firstly: Is the return transmission effect between stock markets and commodity futures the same for both periods? Secondly, do the shocks which occur in the variance of returns spillover across markets in both periods? Thirdly, does the correlation among these markets change during the period of the COVID-19 crisis? Fourthly, what are the average weights of S&amp;P 500 and commodities, and do the optimal weights have different values for the two eras? Fifthly, should hedging the stock market with commodity futures be preferred, or vice versa? Sixthly, do the hedging ratios change over these periods? Seventhly, has COVID-19 caused an upsurge in the downside risk of these markets? Eighthly, can the DCC-MEGARCH approach evaluate the exact VaR of these markets? The answers to these questions would enable policy-makers, investors, and academicians to heighten their awareness of the risk configuration of these markets and make more timely decisions involving the stock markets and commodities. According to the authors' best information, no research deals with these markets during the standard era and COVID-19 period in a single article. The findings of this inquiry are significant for speculators, financial management firms, and policy-makers.</span></p><p>The remainder of this study is organized as follows: Section <a href="#sec2"><span><span>2 Literature review</span></span></a>, <a href="#sec3"><span><span>3 Data and methodology</span></span></a> deal with the literature review and data and methodology, respectively; Section <a href="#sec4"><span><span>4</span></span></a> presents comprehensive results; Section <a href="#sec5"><span><span>5</span></span></a> concludes.</p></section><section><h2>2. Literature review</h2><p>The COVID-19 has significantly damaged the economy of the globe. Keeping in view the scope COVID-19 crisis, the dynamic association among stock markets and commodities have gained significant attention from researchers due to their significance to portfolio diversification. For instance, <a href="#bib41"><span><span>Okorie and Lin (2021)</span></span></a><span> analyzed the <a href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/economics-econometrics-and-finance/contagion-effect">contagion effect</a> during the COVID-19 pandemic. The study found significant evidence of the contagion effect in the stock markets due to COVID-19. Particularly, an investigation documented that these effects were noted in both returns and volatility of stock markets. </span><a href="#bib40"><span><span>Nguyen et al. (2021)</span></span></a><span> evaluated the contagion effect and variance <a href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/economics-econometrics-and-finance/spillover-effect">spillover effect</a> from the US and Chinese stocks to other financial markets of the world. The outcomes of the study revealed that there was a significant contagion effect from the Chinese and the US stock markets to global stock markets; nevertheless, the study found weak evidence of variance spillover effect among them.</span></p><p><a href="#bib26"><span><span>Kang and Lee (2019)</span></span></a> examine the dynamic volatility spillovers and network connectivity between stock index and commodity futures markets using the multivariate DECO-FIGARCH model and the spillover index method. The estimation findings show a positive equivalence between the Index and commodity futures. In addition, it is seen that the highest spillover was during the 2008–2009 global financial crisis and the 2010–2012 European debt crisis. <a href="#bib3"><span><span>An et al. (2020)</span></span></a> reveal the magnitude and direction of spreads in nineteen bulk of metal futures prices using a network theory approach that includes a bivariate spillover model. According to the findings, energy bulk mineral commodities such as natural gas acts as the highest net spillover transmitter, while an industrial metal commodity such as US Steel acts as the net highest spillover receiver. <a href="#bib37"><span><span>Mensi et al. (2021)</span></span></a> examine the short-, medium- and long-term volatility spillovers between developed and developing countries for equity markets and strategic commodity futures markets. The results provide evidence of time-varying volatility spillovers that intensify under major events. (ii) Moreover, overall volatility spillovers are higher in the short run than in the medium and long run. (iii) A mixed portfolio provides a higher level of hedging effectiveness for both emerging and developed markets.</p><p><a href="#bib52"><span><span>Vo and Hung (2021)</span></span></a> examined the volatility spillovers effect among the gold, crude oil, and S&amp;P 500 index. The investigation used the wavelet approach and found that the returns transmission pattern is more thickened during the COVID-19 era than the pre-COVID-19 era. <a href="#bib53"><span><span>Wen et al. (2021)</span></span></a> evaluated the spillovers impact between the Chinese commodities and Chinese stock markets. The investigation used the TVP-VAR approach and found that Chinese stock markets are the net recipient of the variance spillovers effect. The study nominated that volatility association among these markets becomes more critical after a major crisis. Also, the study stated that the hedging properties of Chinese commodity markets have been weakened during the COVID-19 pandemic era. <a href="#bib22"><span><span>Erdem (2020)</span></span></a> analyzed the adverse effects of COVID-19 on the stock markets of independent and less independent nations. The findings expressed that financial markets of partially independent nations have been affected more because of the same size of COVID-19 cases. <a href="#bib1"><span><span>Adekoya and Oliyide (2021)</span></span></a> employed the time-varying parameter vector autoregression approach (TVP-VAR) to ascertain the spillovers effect among commodity and stock markets. The examination confirmed that gold is the significant receiver of the shocks transmitted from the financial markets. The study employed a quantile-based linear and non-linear causality test. The verdicts of this test announce that COVID-19 is greatly responsible for volatility transmission among the stock markets and commodity markets.</p><p><a href="#bib23"><span><span>Farid et al. (2021)</span></span></a> inspected stock markets (S&amp;P 500) and commodity markets of the US, i.e., natural gas, gold, oil, and silver by utilizing the MCS-GARCH and <a href="#bib55"><span><span>Diebold and Yilmaz (2012)</span></span></a> volatility index model. The investigation explores the behavior of risk transmission among these markets before and during the COVID-19 pandemic era. The results showed that volatility connectedness among these markets was at peaked during the COVID-19 era. Notably, the stock market and gold is the significant contributor of variance shocks to the volatility connectedness system. <a href="#bib38"><span><span>Narayan et al. (2020)</span></span></a><span> articulated the association between depreciation of the Yen (Japanese currency) and the stock markets of Japan. The investigation unrevealed that depreciation of the Japanese Yen against the US dollar imperatively increases the <a href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/economics-econometrics-and-finance/capital-market-returns">stock returns</a> during the COVID-19 period (January 2020–August 2020). </span><a href="#bib45"><span><span>Salisu et al. (2020)</span></span></a> employed the panel vector autoregressive model to discover the oil-stock affiliation during the COVID-19 era. The investigation discovered that both stock markets and oil returns have faced long-term shocks happened due to their own and cross volatility effects. Similarly, <a href="#bib49"><span><span>Shehzad et al. (2021b)</span></span></a> estimated the impact of COVID-19 and the global financial crisis on the Dow Jones index and WTI crude oil returns. The study employed the E-GARCH model and found that the variance of both markets is high during the COVID-19 era in comparison with the global financial crisis of 2007–2009. Moreover, the study exposed that the trade war between China and the US showed an insignificant impact on the returns of both markets.</p><p><a href="#bib29"><span><span>Kinateder et al. (2021)</span></span></a><span> used to DCC-GARCH model to evaluate the safe-haven properties of gold and government sovereign bonds. The investigation elaborated that sovereign bonds of the UK, US, and Germany have significant <a href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/economics-econometrics-and-finance/safe-haven">safe haven</a> properties for properties. </span><a href="#bib5"><span><span>Ashraf (2020)</span></span></a> analyzed the response of the stock markets to the COVID-19 cases and deaths that happened due to COVID-19. The investigation confirmed that stock market returns goes down when the COVID-19 cases increased. Moreover, the study documented that COVID-19 cases have a greater negative impact than COVID-19 deaths on the stock market's returns. Similarly, <a href="#bib47"><span><span>Shehzad et al., (2020a)</span></span></a> analyzed the impact of the health crisis generated due to COVID-19 on the stock markets of the US and European nations. The investigation announced that COVID-19 deaths have significantly affected the Nasdaq composite index returns. However, increases in COVID-19 cases were proved to be more dangerous for S&amp;P 500 returns. Hence, the above-summarized literature exposed that COVID-19 has imperatively affected the stock markets and commodity markets. Consequently, it becomes important to explore the time-varying correlation and volatility spillovers among the stock markets and commodities; additionally, to find suitable hedging opportunities and safe haven for investors during the COVID-19 period.</p></section><section><h2>3. Data and methodology</h2><section><h3>3.1. Data</h3><p>This examination is based on the sample period January 04, 2010, to May 10, 2020, divided into two sections, segment A and segment B. In the analysis, segment A represents the stable period (January 04, 2010, to November 30, 2019), while segment B represents the COVID-19 era (December 1, 2019, to May 10, 2020).<a href="#fn3"><span><span><sup>3</sup></span></span></a><span> The data used in the analysis is collected from the database of the US Energy Information Administration, Investing. Com, and Yahoo <a href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/economics-econometrics-and-finance/finance">Finance</a>.</span></p></section><section><h3>3.2. Methodology</h3><p>This investigation computes daily returns as follows:<span><span><span>(1)</span><span><span></span><span><span></span></span></span></span></span>where <span><span></span><span><span></span></span></span> is the day-wise returns of the study variables at time t, and <span><span></span><span><span></span></span></span>, and <span><span></span><span><span></span></span></span> are the daily prices of the study variables at time t and t-1, respectively. There are numerous multivariate GARCH (MGARCH) models, e.g., the diagonal and full vector-half (VECH) models(<a href="#bib10"><span><span>Bollerslev et al., 1988</span></span></a>) and the BEKK-GARCH model (<a href="#bib21"><span><span>Engle and Kroner, 1995</span></span></a>), which deal with returns and volatility transmission impacts. <a href="#bib44"><span><span>Sadorsky (2012)</span></span></a> contends that these models have various restrictions such as complications estimating the parameters, and poor performance of the likelihood function (for more detail, see <a href="#bib8"><span><span>Bauwens et al. (2006)</span></span></a>). In order to find evidence of the risk transmission effect, this study applies the dynamic conditional correlation (DCC) multivariate exponential generalized autoregressive conditional heteroskedasticity (MEGARCH) model introduced by <a href="#bib19"><span><span>Engle (2002)</span></span></a><span>. It amalgamates the DCC-MEGARCH model with the vector autoregressive (VAR) model to capture evidence of returns transmission from one market to another. The DCC-MEGARCH model has several advantages over other GARCH models. Firstly, the standard MGARCH model ignores the parametric change in the returns and computes instability through the magnitude of <a href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/economics-econometrics-and-finance/capital-market-returns">stock returns</a>. However, the MEGARCH approach follows a parametric method to ascertain conditional variance. Secondly, the standard MGARCH model cannot evaluate the exact volatility persistence in the market (</span><a href="#bib39"><span><span>Nelson, 1991</span></span></a>). Although MEGARCH does not impose non-negativity conditions on the parameters, it significantly evaluates stock returns' asymmetric behavior (<a href="#bib50"><span><span>Sikhosana and Aye, 2018</span></span></a>). On the other hand, the correlation between two securities has a domineering role in managing these markets' financial risk and needs a wide range of correlation series (<a href="#bib9"><span><span>BenSaïda et al., 2018</span></span></a><span>). Similarly, portfolio optimization requires the covariance and <a href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/economics-econometrics-and-finance/measure-of-dispersion">standard deviation</a> information of financial assets. The DCC-MEGARCH approach calculates the time-varying correlation between financial assets. It first analyses the univariate GARCH model then computes the time-varying conditional correlation among returns (</span><a href="#bib19"><span><span>Engle, 2002</span></span></a>). In the DCC-GARCH model, the number of parameters evaluated does not hinge on the number of variables. Hence, a large number of correlations can be computed at a time (<a href="#bib19"><span><span>Engle, 2002</span></span></a>).</p><section><h4>3.2.1. Similarly, when <em>i</em> <span><span></span><span><span></span></span></span> <em>0</em>, it estimates the returns transmission impact from asset j to asset <em>i</em>. Conversely, if <em>i</em> = <em>j</em>, it characterizes the lagged returns' transmission effect on its subsequent returns. Additionally, <span><span></span><span><span></span></span></span> and <span><span></span><span><span></span></span></span> are the error term and number of variables, respectively.</p></section><section><h4>3.2.2. Computation of the DCC-MEGARCH model</h4><p>This examination evaluates the variance equation through the MEGARCH (1, 1) model-based onthe Akaike information criterion as follows:<span><span><span>(3)</span><span><span></span><span><span></span></span></span></span></span>where <span><span></span><span><span></span></span></span> and <span><span></span><span><span></span></span></span> arethe variance term and constant of series <em>i</em> at time t, respectively. If <em>i</em> <span><span></span><span><span></span></span></span> <em>j,</em> <span><span></span><span><span></span></span></span> represents a parameter that captures the volatility spillover effect from series <em>j</em> to <em>i</em>. Moreover, in the case where <em>i</em> = <em>j</em>, it calculates the influence of shocks in the return series <em>i</em> at time t, on its own next-day variance, i.e. the ARCH effect. Also, <span><span></span><span><span></span></span></span> isthe impact of variations occurring in the variance of series <em>i</em> at time t on its own next-day variance, i.e. the GARCH effect.
