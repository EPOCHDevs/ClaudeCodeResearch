For this purpose, we suggest a new multivariate volatility model based on open, high, low, and closing prices that incorporates the impact of investor attention on returns, volatility, and covariances. Introduction</h2><p>This paper studies the impact of investor attention to oil price, as measured by Google searches for “oil prices” on returns, volatility and covariance of three exchange traded funds representing oil, gold, and the stock market. Our paper unites two strands of literature: 1) the impact of investor attention on the oil market and 2) the comovement between the oil market and other markets.</p><p>Crude oil is the world's most important commodity, and understanding the oil market and its risks is therefore important (<a href="#bb0160">Hung et al., 2008</a>; <a href="#bb0165">Hung et al., 2011</a>; <a href="#bb0125">Gkillas et al., 2020</a>; <a href="#bb0130">Gkillas et al., 2021a</a>; <a href="#bb0305">Tiwari et al., 2020</a>). It has been long recognized that investor attention has an influence on financial markets, see e.g. <a href="#bb0075">Da et al. (2011)</a>. Attention can be measured in various ways, for example from news articles or social networks such as twitter (<a href="#bb0120">Gjerstad et al., 2021</a>). One particularly useful data source to estimate attention is Google Trends. Google is the largest search engine in the world, and information regarding how much people search for a particular topic in Google is therefore a very relevant measure of attention.</p><p>It is important to emphasize that the channel between attention and financial markets is indirect. People often search for additional information before they make decisions, including trading decisions. In other words, the act of searching for information often precedes the actual action. This way, attention estimated from Google Trends might precede actual trading, and therefore might predict returns and volatility in various financial markets. In other words, it is not searching on Google that moves the markets, but information about Google searches could predict subsequent movements in the markets.</p><p>Researchers have also studied the impact of attention measured from Google Trends on the oil market. The most common applications are the impact of investor attention on oil price (<a href="#bb0220">Li et al., 2015</a>; <a href="#bb0340">Yao et al., 2017</a>; <a href="#bb0085">Elshendy et al., 2018</a>; <a href="#bb0225">Li et al., 2019</a>; <a href="#bb0230">Li et al., 2020</a>; <a href="#bb0335">Yang et al., 2021</a>; <a href="#bb0055">Chen et al., 2022</a>), the influence on oil price volatility (<a href="#bb0010">Afkhami et al., 2017</a>; <a href="#bb0045">Campos et al., 2017</a>; <a href="#bb0325">Wang et al., 2018</a>; <a href="#bb0330">Xiao and Wang, 2021</a>; <a href="#bb0235">Liu et al., 2022</a>), or the impact of investor attention on both oil price and oil price volatility (<a href="#bb0140">Guo and Ji, 2013</a>; <a href="#bb0170">Ji and Guo, 2015</a>; <a href="#bb0270">Qadan and Nama, 2018</a>). Review of these articles is provided in the next section.</p><p>Similarly, there is a large volume of literature about comovement between oil and other commodities (<a href="#bb0015">Ahmadi et al., 2016</a>; <a href="#bb0020">Bašta and Molnár, 2018</a>; <a href="#bb0025">Behmiri et al., 2019</a>). However, research about the impact of investor attention on the comovement (covariance) between oil price and other assets is very limited. To the best of our knowledge, the only study on this topic is <a href="#bb0265">Prange (2021)</a>. This novel study finds that online investor attention is a statistically significant determinant of the time-varying correlations between oil and other assets.</p><p>The main difference between <a href="#bb0265">Prange (2021)</a> and our study is that <a href="#bb0265">Prange (2021)</a> use a volatility model based on closing prices, while we suggest a new volatility model based on open, high, low, and closing prices. Overwhelmingly, multivariate volatility models are based on closing prices. Meanwhile, the incorporation of low and high prices for such models can both improve the estimation of variances and covariances and increase their forecasting accuracy (see e.g. <a href="#bb0065">Chou et al., 2009</a>; <a href="#bb0300">Su and Wu, 2014</a>; <a href="#bb0095">Fiszeder and Fałdziński, 2019</a>; <a href="#bb0105">Fiszeder et al., 2019</a>; <a href="#bb0080">De Nard et al., 2022</a>; <a href="#bb0100">Fiszeder and Małecka, 2022</a>; <a href="#bb0110">Fiszeder et al., 2023</a>). Low and high prices are generally available with closing prices and can be easily utilized.</p><p>Similarly to <a href="#bb0265">Prange (2021)</a>, we use the extended dynamic conditional correlation (DCC) model of <a href="#bb0090">Engle (2002)</a> to incorporate the influence of exogenous variables in the dynamic conditional correlation. There are several possible ways to introduce the influence of exogenous variables on correlations (see <a href="#bb0285">Schopen, 2012</a>). <a href="#bb0295">Silvennoinen and Teräsvirta (2005)</a> introduced the smooth transition conditional correlation model for which time-varying conditional correlations change smoothly between two regimes driven by an exogenous variable. <a href="#bb0290">Sheppard (2008)</a> assumed that the symmetric square root of the <a href="/topics/engineering/covariance-matrix">covariance matrix</a> is a function of exogenous variables. <a href="#bb0060">Chou and Liao (2008)</a> and <a href="#bb0315">Vargas (2008)</a> extended the DCC model of <a href="#bb0090">Engle (2002)</a> to incorporate the influence of exogenous variables and introduced the dynamic conditional correlation with exogenous variables model (DCC-X). <a href="#bb0245">Min and Hwang (2012)</a> and <a href="#bb0190">Kim et al. (2013)</a> introduced the bivariate DCC-X model where the conditional correlation coefficient is determined by exogenous variables. In this paper, we explore the DCC-X model of <a href="#bb0315">Vargas (2008)</a> because it is quite general (not limited to a bivariate case) but at the same time it is relatively easy to apply.</p><p>We analyze three exchange-traded funds: United States Oil Fund, SPDR Portfolio S&amp;P 500 Growth and SPDR Gold Shares and confront the proposed model with three competing models: the DCC model of <a href="#bb0090">Engle (2002)</a>, the DCC-X model of <a href="#bb0315">Vargas (2008)</a> and the DCC-RGARCH model of <a href="#bb0105">Fiszeder et al. (2019)</a>. An application of the alternative models is for benchmark purposes and used here to assess the importance of additional information from Google searches and the range-based model framework.</p><p>This study has two main contributions. The first one is a proposition of the new DCC-X-RGARCH-X model. It is an extension of the DCC-RGARCH model of <a href="#bb0105">Fiszeder et al. (2019)</a> which incorporates exogenous variables in the equations of conditional means, variances and covariances. Second, we utilize this model, and demonstrate that Google searches for “oil prices” can explain and forecast covariances between the oil exchange-traded fund and exchange traded funds representing the stock market and gold. Moreover, the DCC-X-RGARCH-X model, which incorporates Google searches for “oil prices” as exogeneous variable, outperform other considered multivariate volatility models.</p><p>The rest of the paper is organized in the following way. <a href="#s0010">Section 2</a> reviews the related literature. <a href="#s0015">Section 3</a> presents the proposed DCC-X-RGARCH-X model. <a href="#s0020">Section 4</a> describes the data used in the research. In <a href="#s0025">Section 5</a> the influence of Google searches on volatility and dependence for the selected ETFs is analyzed. In <a href="#s0045">Section 6</a>, we perform a robustness check for the inclusion of the COVID-19 crisis. <a href="#s0050">Section 7</a> provides conclusions.</p></section><section><h2>2. Literature review</h2><p>In this section, we review papers that studied the impact of attention on crude oil price and its volatility.</p><p><a href="#bb0220">Li et al. (2015)</a> measure attention from Google Trends for “oil price”. They utilize <a href="/topics/economics-econometrics-and-finance/causality-analysis">Granger causality</a> and regressions and conclude that Google searches for “oil price” capture the attention of non-professional traders, and there is a feedback loop between attention and crude oil price.</p><p><a href="#bb0340">Yao et al. (2017)</a> obtain Google searches for “oil price”, “current oil prices”, “price per barrel”, “Bloomberg energy”, “oil price per barrel”, “current oil”, “crude oil chart”, “crude oil”, “current crude oil”, “current crude oil price”, and “current crude” and use principal component analysis to construct the investor attention measure. They employ the structural vector <a href="/topics/engineering/autoregression-model">autoregression model</a> and find that investor attention has a significant negative impact on oil prices and contributes 15% to the long-run fluctuation of the oil prices.</p><p><a href="#bb0085">Elshendy et al. (2018)</a> study whether information extracted from Twitter, Google Trends, Wikipedia, and the Global Data on Events, Location and Tone database can improve the forecasts of the crude oil price. They use <a href="/topics/economics-econometrics-and-finance/arma-model">autoregressive integrated moving average</a> with explanatory variable models and find that the information combined from these platforms contains a valuable information for forecasting.</p><p><a href="#bb0225">Li et al. (2019)</a> construct the investor attention measure from Google searches for “crude oil”, “Brent” and “petroleum”. They utilize nonlinear <a href="/topics/economics-econometrics-and-finance/granger-causality-test">Granger causality tests</a> and find bidirectional causality between investor attention and crude oil returns. However, this Granger causality is stronger from investor attention to returns.</p><p><a href="#bb0230">Li et al. (2020)</a> also use Google Trends to construct attention measure. However, they combine searches for “oil price” in the English language with equivalent searches also in Arabic, Spanish, Portuguese, Japanese, German and Persian. As methods, they use not only regressions, but also <a href="/topics/engineering/machine-learning-technique">machine learning techniques</a> such as neural networks. They find that Google Trends attention improves crude oil price predictions, and the multilingual attention measure works better than single-language attention measure.</p><p><a href="#bb0335">Yang et al. (2021)</a> utilize Google searches for 40 oil-related search terms, such as “oil price”, “oil demand” or “oil supply”, together with various economic variables. They use machine learning techniques for the dimension reduction of input variables and also for the forecasting of crude oil price. They conclude that Google Trends data is useful in oil price forecasting and that machine learning “divide and conquer” techniques work well in forecasting.</p><p><a href="#bb0055">Chen et al. (2022)</a> use Google Trends to obtain 17 oil-related search terms, such as “WTI” or “petrol price”. Attention is constructed from these using principal component analysis. They employ wavelet <a href="/topics/engineering/coherency">coherency</a>, causality-in-quantile, and quantile-on-quantile regression methods to study the linkage between investor attention and crude oil across time and frequency domains. They find that in most cases, attention is negatively correlated with the crude oil market. However, the impact of attention on crude oil differs across quantiles, and it becomes greatest under extreme market conditions.</p><p><a href="#bb0010">Afkhami et al. (2017)</a> study volatility of not only crude oil, but also conventional gasoline, heating oil, and natural gas. They obtain 90 oil- and energy-related keywords from Google Trends and use Granger causality tests to choose relevant search terms. They use the GARCH model and find that attention improves volatility models for these energy commodities.</p><p><a href="#bb0045">Campos et al. (2017)</a> use Google searches for “oil prices” as a proxy for investor attention to oil and study whether it can predict the implied volatility of oil. They use regressions as their method and find that attention is useful in predicting the implied volatility of oil even if traditional financial and macro variables are taken into account. Moreover, attention has economic value, allowing traders of volatility-exposed portfolios to increase returns.</p><p><a href="#bb0325">Wang et al. (2018)</a> combine Google searches for “crude oil”, “oil price”, “crude oil price”, and “crude oil prices”. This variable is used in <a href="/topics/engineering/extreme-learning-machine">extreme machine learning</a> models. They conclude that Google Trends is a useful tool for quantifying investor attention that can help predict volatility in the oil market.</p><p><a href="#bb0330">Xiao and Wang (2021)</a> also use Google Trends to estimate sentiment. To ensure robustness, they consider searches for “oil price”, “oil prices”, and “crude oil”. They decompose volatility into good and bad volatility, and using regressions, they find that changes in investor attention mainly affect bad volatility rather than good volatility.</p><p><a href="#bb0235">Liu et al. (2022)</a> obtain Google Trends data for 519 keywords and then use backwards regression to select the final 83 search terms. They further utilize 5-min high-frequency oil price data to construct daily volatilities. The considered models (heterogeneous <a href="/topics/economics-econometrics-and-finance/autoregressive-model">autoregressive model</a> and heterogeneous autoregressive model with Markov-switching) perform better when attention is included in these models.</p><p><a href="#bb0140">Guo and Ji (2013)</a> use Google Trends to obtain market concerns for oil price and market concerns for oil demand. They use cointegration and the exponential <a href="/topics/economics-econometrics-and-finance/generalized-autoregressive-conditional-heteroskedasticity">generalized autoregressive conditional heteroskedasticity</a> (EGARCH) model and conclude that there is a long-term equilibrium between oil prices and long-run market concerns.</p><p><a href="#bb0170">Ji and Guo (2015)</a> utilize data from Google Trends in their study of the impact of oil-related events (hurricanes, <a href="/topics/engineering/global-financial-crisis">global financial crisis</a>, Libyan war, OPEC conference) on oil price and its volatility. They find that responses vary across events. The impact of the global financial crisis on the oil price returns was significantly negative, the impact of the Libyan war and hurricanes was significantly positive, while reactions to OPEC production announcements were inconsistent.</p><p><a href="#bb0270">Qadan and Nama (2018)</a> utilize an extensive list of variables capturing investor sentiment and find that this sentiment has a significant effect on oil prices. Moreover, volatility in these sentiment indices spills over and can explain part of the oil price volatility. Regarding the Google searches for “oil price”, “oil prices”, or “crude oil”, they find a bidirectional relationship between these searches and oil price volatility.</p></section><section><h2>3.
