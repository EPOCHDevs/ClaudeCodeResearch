<a href="https://jfin-swufe.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s40854-024-00732-1#Fn1"><span>Footnote </span>1</a><a href="https://jfin-swufe.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s40854-024-00732-1#Fn2"><span>Footnote </span>2</a><p>In an era characterized by increasingly interconnected global financial markets, understanding the dynamics and risk interplay among various assets is essential for portfolio managers, risk managers, and other financial market participants. Emerging economies, in particular, are pivotal players in this arena as they contribute substantially to the world’s economic growth. According to the International Monetary Fund (IMF) 20 emerging economies represent 34% of global nominal Gross Domestic Product (GDP) in US dollar terms and 46% in purchasing power parity terms. Moreover, 24 countries designated as emerging markets constituted 50.1% of global GDP in 2023 and 66% of global GDP growth over the 10&nbsp;years from 2014 to 2023. However, this prominent role for emerging market economies is accompanied by heightened volatility and susceptibility to external shocks. As such, it is important for investors, policymakers, and financial institutions to understand the relationships between key asset classes such as oil, gold, an index of global commodities, and emerging stock markets.</p><p>The global financial crisis (GFC) of 2008 reshaped the financial landscape, prompting a reassessment of traditional investment strategies. The decade-plus following the GFC has seen unprecedented market fluctuations, necessitating a thorough exploration of how various assets react and interact during both normal and turbulent times. In light of this, the current study examines how various market crises alter risk transmission mechanisms and correlations between key asset classes and emerging economies’ stock markets. Several studies investigate the impact of changes in commodity prices on stock market performance (Vardar et al. <a href="https://jfin-swufe.springeropen.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00732-1#ref-CR48">2018</a>; He et al. <a href="https://jfin-swufe.springeropen.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00732-1#ref-CR22">2020</a>; Sarwar et al. <a href="https://jfin-swufe.springeropen.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00732-1#ref-CR44">2020</a>; Mensi et al. <a href="https://jfin-swufe.springeropen.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00732-1#ref-CR33">2021</a>). Recent developments in the financial and commodity markets call for inquiries about the role of commodities as a diversifying asset class and a refuge during stock market turmoil, as stock markets are meeting places for sellers and buyers of stocks.</p><a href="https://jfin-swufe.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s40854-024-00732-1#Fn3"><span>Footnote </span>3</a><p>Despite significant uncertainty in commodity markets, commodity trading generated over $100 billion in 2023, translating to more than $150 billion in gross margin. In today’s environment, future success increasingly depends on the ability to adapt to unpredictable market conditions. The rapid expansion of commodities markets is attracting a variety of new players, including tech-savvy trading firms, hedge funds, banks, and entities involved in mining and processing metals. At the same time, other crisis following the GFC increased investors’ and regulators’ concerns regarding risk contagion and systemic risks. Oil plays a primary role in economies worldwide, regardless of their level of economic development, and is a prominent sector in equity markets. With practically every production sector in the international economy relying heavily on this energy resource, the relationship between crude oil and stock markets has developed over time. Therefore, understanding the impact of variations in crude oil prices is critical as it may create uncertainty in all sectors of the economy and lead to economic stress for both oil-importing and oil-exporting countries (Benlagha et al. <a href="https://jfin-swufe.springeropen.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00732-1#ref-CR7">2022</a>; Naz et al. <a href="https://jfin-swufe.springeropen.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00732-1#ref-CR40">2024</a>).</p><a href="https://jfin-swufe.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s40854-024-00732-1#Fn4"><span>Footnote </span>4</a><a href="https://jfin-swufe.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s40854-024-00732-1#Fn5"><span>Footnote </span>5</a><a href="https://jfin-swufe.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s40854-024-00732-1#Fn6"><span>Footnote </span>6</a><p>Gold continues to play a pivotal role in the financial markets, demonstrating resilience and value across fluctuating economic conditions. In 2023, gold prices reached what was an all-time high of $2,135.39 per ounce, driven by a combination of central bank purchases, geopolitical tensions, and the anticipation of lower interest rates in the US. Meanwhile, gold’s role as a reliable store of value is further reinforced by its performance during periods of easing monetary policy and global crises, such as the COVID-19 pandemic (Karim &amp; Lucey <a href="https://jfin-swufe.springeropen.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00732-1#ref-CR27">2024</a>; Husain et al. <a href="https://jfin-swufe.springeropen.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00732-1#ref-CR24">2024</a>) and geopolitical conflicts such as the Russia-Ukraine war. This has supported increased buying by central banks, especially from countries seeking to diversify away from the US dollar, and heightened retail demand in emerging markets. The World Gold Council noted the total gold supply rose by 3% year-over-year in 2023, driven by increases in mine production and recycling, which responded positively to high gold prices, indicating a robust and responsive global gold market that can meet rising demand.</p><p>The growing interconnectedness of global financial markets amplifies both opportunities and risks. As the world economy becomes more globalized and technological advances enhance cross-border communication and transactions, financial markets have become more closely linked. This not only facilitates capital flows, drives market integration, and fosters cross-border investments; it also heightens the potential for financial contagion and systemic risk. When a shock occurs in one market, its effects can rapidly spread to others, causing destabilizing effects in many economies. The complex linkages between oil, gold, commodities, and stocks—especially in emerging markets—underscore the need to understand these systemic risks. By investigating the dynamics of these interconnections, particularly in the context of financial contagion, this study aims to shed light on vulnerabilities that may arise in an increasingly interconnected financial landscape (Corbet &amp; Goodell <a href="https://jfin-swufe.springeropen.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00732-1#ref-CR13">2022</a>).</p><p>The dynamics of financial market interdependence have garnered significant attention from researchers, policymakers, and investors. This interconnectedness has substantial implications for market efficiency, optimal asset allocation, and financial innovation. As markets become increasingly interdependent, the flow of information and capital across borders can improve market efficiency and create opportunities for diversification and innovation in financial products (Bouzzine &amp; Lueg <a href="https://jfin-swufe.springeropen.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00732-1#ref-CR11">2020</a>; Corbet, et al. <a href="https://jfin-swufe.springeropen.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00732-1#ref-CR14">2022</a>). However, this interconnectedness also presents challenges, notably a heightened vulnerability to systemic risks. The close ties across financial markets mean that shocks in one market can quickly ripple through others, increasing the potential for widespread financial instability. By examining the interdependence of markets such as oil, gold, commodities, and emerging market stocks, we seek to uncover the underlying mechanisms that drive systemic risks, offering important insights for investors and policymakers.</p><p>Peer effects play a critical role in the contagion process, especially in highly interconnected financial markets. These effects occur when the actions and behaviors of market participants are influenced by their peers, creating a cascade of reactions that can amplify the impact of financial shocks. During periods of crisis, such as the GFC or geopolitical conflicts, peer effects becomes even more important. As market participants observe the responses of others they may adjust their own strategies, often leading to a herding behavior that spreads the contagion and exacerbates market volatility (Mugerman et al. <a href="https://jfin-swufe.springeropen.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00732-1#ref-CR38">2014</a>; Mugerman et al. <a href="https://jfin-swufe.springeropen.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00732-1#ref-CR39">2022</a>). By incorporating the concept of peer effects into the analysis of systemic risks across oil, gold, commodities, and stocks in emerging markets, we seek to provide a more complete understanding of how individual and collective behaviors help to propagate market crises. Recognizing the influence of peer effects is essential in developing strategies to mitigate the risks of financial contagion, particularly in markets where the actions of a few can have far-reaching consequences.</p><p>Our goal is to study risk spillovers between commodities (specifically, crude oil, gold and global commodities) and emerging stock markets, using the Asymmetric Dynamic Conditional Correlation–Conditional Value at Risk (ADCC-CoVaR) and the bootstrapped Kolmogorov–Smirnov (KS) methods for the period from December 30, 2005 to February 28, 2024. This period was characterized by three major international disruptions, namely the GFC, the COVID-19 outbreak, and the Russia-Ukraine war.</p><p>This study contributes to the current body of knowledge in various ways. First<i>,</i> in view of the conflicting findings in previous studies, we comprehensively examine risk spillovers between commodities and emerging economies’ stock markets. The asset classes included in the study are global commodities, represented by the Dow Jones Commodity Index (DJCI), oil, represented by West Texas Intermediate (WTI) crude oil<i>,</i> and gold, along with the 100 emerging market stock markets that comprise the Dow Jones Emerging Markets (DJEM) index. The asset classes covered in this study differ from those in previous empirical studies, which do not adequately identify risk transmission between commodities and emerging stock markets. Our analysis takes into account many turbulent periods in global markets, such as the GFC, the COVID-19 pandemic, and the war between Russia and Ukraine, as it is essential to understand how extreme events can have a major influence on these markets. This study also contributes to the literature on the interconnections between financial markets, with important implications for policymakers, regulatory authorities, and market participants.</p><p>The volatility in the stock and commodity markets during the GFC, the COVID-19 pandemic, and the war between Russia and Ukraine motivated this effort to examine the transmission risk between crude oil, gold, global commodities, and emerging stock markets. The analysis is relevant not only under stable market conditions but also in bearish and bullish market scenarios, capturing investors’ heterogeneous risk appetites during market ups and downs.</p><p>The commodities included in this study reflect their importance in global markets. Crude oil is an essential energy commodity that serves as a vital raw material in for power generation and industrial applications worldwide (Yadav et al. <a href="https://jfin-swufe.springeropen.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00732-1#ref-CR56">2023</a>), and plays a crucial role in industrial development. According to Li et al. (<a href="https://jfin-swufe.springeropen.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00732-1#ref-CR28">2021</a>), given oil’s role as a key production input, changes in oil prices have a considerable influence on the global economy and financial markets. Gold is included as investors use it to manage downside risk and diversify their holdings, especially during periods of uncertainty (Thai Hung <a href="https://jfin-swufe.springeropen.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00732-1#ref-CR47">2024</a>). Various economic agents, market analysts, policymakers, researchers, and others are interested in the interaction between commodities (especially crude oil and gold) and stock markets (Mensi et al. <a href="https://jfin-swufe.springeropen.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00732-1#ref-CR33">2021</a>).</p><p>The choice to focus on emerging markets in this study is based on the observation that these markets share several distinctive characteristics that highlight the importance of these markets as an asset class. Furthermore, and emerging market stocks have been integrated into international and specialized investment portfolios, gaining importance due to their diversification benefits. Thus, it is useful to understand emerging market stocks and their relationship to commodities markets. We also note that the COVID-19 pandemic has affected many economic sectors and stock markets, causing significant fluctuations in financial markets around the world. However, as financial markets in emerging economies are typically underdeveloped, the adverse effects of crises on the stock markets of these economies may persist longer than in developed markets, even during the subsequent recovery in developed economies (Hashmi et al. <a href="https://jfin-swufe.springeropen.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00732-1#ref-CR21">2021</a>).</p><p>Second, to the authors’ best knowledge, ours is the first study to use the ADCC-CoVaR and the bootstrapped two-sample KS test to examine dynamic dependence and risk spillovers between commodities and emerging stock markets. Using the Asymmetric Dynamic Conditional Correlations (ADCC) model in conjunction with the Conditional Value-at-Risk (CoVaR) methodology represents a cutting-edge approach to highlighting the complexities of financial risk management. ADCC allows us to assess dynamic correlations, acknowledging that market relationships evolve over time, which is particularly crucial in the context of emerging markets where the interconnectedness of assets can fluctuate substantially, especially during and after major financial disruptions. Similarly, the asymmetry accommodated by the ADCC model is of paramount importance, given that markets often react differently to positive versus negative shocks. The ADCC captures this, providing a more realistic and specific representation of risk dynamics. Capturing this asymmetry is essential for investors and policymakers seeking to understand not only how assets move together but also how they may respond asymmetrically to various market conditions. Simply put, ADCC enriches our understanding of risk interplay in a way that traditional, symmetric models may overlook.</p><p>Third, the bootstrapped two-sample KS test adds a layer of robustness to the study’s findings. In financial markets, where uncertainties and non-normalities abound, traditional statistical tests may fall short. The bootstrapped two-sample KS test addresses this by providing a nonparametric approach that does not rely on strict distributional assumptions. Employing the KS method means our study’s conclusions are grounded in statistically sound evidence. By resampling with replacement, bootstrapping, allows us to create multiple samples, reducing the impact of outliers and enhancing the reliability of the statistical tests. This approach is particularly useful when dealing with financial data, which often exhibits non-normal distributions with many outliers during periods of extreme market conditions.</p><p>Fourth, moving beyond entire cumulative distributions, we explore risk spillover significance below the 25th and above the 75th quantiles, examining downside and upside risk spillovers between crude oil, gold, and the global commodities index, and emerging stock markets. Examining the risk spillover significance below the 25th quantile focuses on adverse market conditions, going beyond a more generic understanding of risk and uncovering vulnerabilities that may be overlooked when considering the entire distribution. Identifying asymmetric responses to negative shocks seen in these lower quantiles provides essential insights into how assets react and transmit risk when markets are under stress, with a level of granularity that offers a targeted understanding of downside risk dynamics during challenging economic periods. Conversely, by investigating risk spillovers above the 75th quantile we shed light on market conditions associated with positive dynamics and growth. Identifying asymmetries in risk transmission in these higher quantiles is equally important as it offers insights into how assets respond during favorable market conditions, unveiling opportunities and highlighting the resilience of the financial system. Therefore, this study is useful for market participants seeking to capitalize on asymmetric market trends and policymakers aiming to foster economic stability.</p><p>This study also extends the body of financial research by using advanced statistical methods to uncover novel interactions between commodities and emerging stock markets, an area that has been underexplored in empirical research. Specifically, employing the ADCC-CoVaR model offers a pioneering approach to understanding how dynamic and asymmetric risk correlations evolve over time to capture the subtle and often overlooked effects of positive versus negative market shocks on interconnected financial systems, offering a more complete picture of risk dynamics. By applying the bootstrapped two-sample KS test, our study addresses a gap in financial econometrics by validating the robustness of results without relying on parametric assumptions, which is particularly relevant in financial contexts where data often display non-normal distributions and are prone to outliers, especially during periods of market turmoil. Additionally, the study’s focus on quantile-based risk spillovers during extreme market conditions provides new insights into the asymmetric risk profiles of financial markets during both crises and growth phases, clarifying how and why markets react differently under varying stress conditions, which is invaluable in developing effective risk management strategies.</p><p>The remainder of this study is structured as follows: Section&nbsp;“<a href="https://jfin-swufe.springeropen.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00732-1#Sec2">Literature review</a>” provides a literature review focused on the behavior of commodity markets and stock indices. Section&nbsp;“<a href="https://jfin-swufe.springeropen.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00732-1#Sec3">Methodology</a>” explains the methodology employed in our study and Sect.&nbsp;“<a href="https://jfin-swufe.springeropen.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00732-1#Sec7">Data and descriptive analysis</a>” presents the data and descriptive analysis. Section&nbsp;“<a href="https://jfin-swufe.springeropen.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00732-1#Sec10">Empirical results</a>” provides the empirical results, while Sect.&nbsp;”<a href="https://jfin-swufe.springeropen.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00732-1#Sec17">Discussion</a>” discusses those results. Finally, conclusions and implications are presented in Sect.&nbsp;“<a href="https://jfin-swufe.springeropen.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00732-1#Sec18">Conclusions and implications</a>”.</p>
