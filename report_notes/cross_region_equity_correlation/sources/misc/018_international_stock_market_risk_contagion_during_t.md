<div><div><main><article><section></section><section><section><h2>Abstract</h2><p>This paper examines the risk contagion among international stock markets during the COVID-19 pandemic by using the realized volatility information from sixteen major stock markets in the world. The empirical evidence based on the connectedness methods of Diebold and Yilmaz (2012) and Baruník and Křehlík (2018) shows that the COVID-19 epidemic significantly increases the risk contagion effects in international stock markets. Besides, the risk spillovers from stock markets in European and American regions increase rapidly but those in Asian markets decrease obviously after the outbreak of COVID-19 pandemic. Finally, the risk contagion among international stock markets caused by the pandemic can last for about 6 to 8 months. These results provide important implications regarding to financial risk management and macroprudential design.<section><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> Risk contagion, COVID-19 pandemic, Spillover, International stock markets</section></section><section><h2>1. Introduction</h2><p>The COVID-19 pandemic deeply affects global economy activities (<a href="/pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov#bib0020">Janiak et al., 2021</a>; <a href="/pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov#bib0038">Tisdell, 2020</a>; <a href="/pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov#bib0045">Yang et al., 2021</a>). Governments have released many precautionary measures (such as city lockdown, flight ban and work suspension) to prevent the further spread of the COVID-19 epidemic, which result in heavy global economy recession and huge fluctuations in international stock markets (<a href="/pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov#bib0008">Contessi and De Pace, 2021</a>; <a href="/pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov#bib0015">Frezza et al., 2021</a>), as well as stronger market risk contagion (spillover effects) among them (<a href="/pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov#bib0012">Davidovic, 2021</a>; <a href="/pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov#bib0043">Wei et al., 2019</a>).<p>Recent studies about the impacts of the COVID-19 epidemic on financial contagion have been very popular (<a href="/pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov#bib0002">Bai et al., 2020</a>, <a href="/pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov#bib0003">Bai et al., 2021</a>; <a href="/pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov#bib0018">Goodell, 2020</a>; <a href="/pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov#bib0027">Liang et al., 2021</a>, <a href="/pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov#bib0028">2020a</a>). Several researchers (<a href="/pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov#bib0001">Akhtaruzzaman et al., 2021</a>; <a href="/pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov#bib0006">Bouri et al., 2021</a>; <a href="/pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov#bib0009">Corbet et al., 2020</a>; <a href="/pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov#bib0016">Gharib et al., 2021</a>; <a href="/pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov#bib0022">Le et al., 2021</a>) find that the correlations among various financial markets increase significantly during the COVID-19 pandemic. For example, <a href="/pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov#bib0019">Guo et al. (2021)</a> indicate that the COVID-19 epidemic enlarges contagion channels in the international financial system. <a href="/pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov#bib0025">Li et al. (2021b)</a> use the causal forest and complex network methods to analyze the characteristics of industry risk contagion before and after the pandemic. <a href="/pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov#bib0008">Contessi and De Pace (2021)</a> find the evidence of instable transmission from the Chinese stock market to other stock markets. <a href="/pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov#bib0036">Sharif et al. (2020)</a> employ wavelet method and unveil the unprecedented impact of COVID-19 on the geopolitical risk, economic policy uncertainty and stock market volatility over the low frequency bands. <a href="/pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov#bib0012">Davidovic (2021)</a> indicates international financial markets are more integrated and risk contagion increases during the pandemic. <a href="/pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov#bib0014">Fassas (2020)</a> investigates the dynamic connectedness of variance risk premium among developed and emerging stock markets and calculates the total investors' risk aversion connectedness.<p>Investigating the international risk contagion is of great importance for international investors to allocate their portfolios and for policy makers to propose relevant policies (<a href="/pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov#bib0004">Bai et al., 2019</a>; <a href="/pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov#bib0010">Coroneoet al., 2020</a>; <a href="/pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov#bib0011">Daly et al., 2019</a>; <a href="/pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov#bib0017">Gkillas et al., 2019</a>; <a href="/pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov#bib0023">Li and Wei, 2018</a>; <a href="/pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov#bib0024">Li et al., 2021a</a>; <a href="/pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov#bib0034">Park and Shin, 2020</a>; <a href="/pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov#bib0039">Wang et al., 2021</a>). However, there are few researches focusing on the risk contagion effects among international stock markets during the COVID-19 epidemic by using the realized volatility (RV) information in these markets.<p>For highly liquid stocks, using RV as market risk measurement has clear advantages. First of all, RV is a nonparametric estimator of intraday price fluctuations (<a href="/pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov#bib0033">Nielsen and Shephard, 2002</a>), and it is very easy to calculate and free from functional form assumptions. Secondly, the distribution of daily logarithm RV appears to be very close to normal distribution, which means that it meets most of assumptions in risk analyzing models (e.g., GARCH-type or HAR-type volatility models). Thirdly, RV measurement ignores the overnight variations in stock price and sometimes the variations in the first few minutes of the trading day when recorded prices may contain large errors (<a href="/pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov#bib0029">Liang et al., 2020b</a>, <a href="/pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov#bib0030">2019</a>; <a href="/pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov#bib0032">Ma et al., 2018</a>; <a href="/pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov#bib0040">Wei et al., 2020</a>, <a href="/pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov#bib0041">Wei et al., 2021</a>). Thus, based on the connectedness methods of <a href="/pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov#bib0013">Diebold and Yilmaz (2012)</a> and <a href="/pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov#bib0005">Baruník and Křehlík (2018)</a>, this paper aims to explore the risk contagion effects across international stock markets by utilizing the RV measurements released by Oxford-Man Institute of Quantitative Finance, and provide some implications relative to financial risk management.</section><section><h2>2. Conclusions</h2><p>This paper investigates risk contagion among sixteen major stock markets across three continents, and compares the spillover effects before and after the outbreak of COVID-19 pandemic in both time and frequency domains. The major empirical results show that, firstly, after the burst of COVID-19 pandemic at the beginning of 2020, the integration of international stock markets enhances greatly and the market risk contagion among them also increases significantly. Therefore, in order to maintain international stock markets stability, policy makers should focus on the risk spillovers directions and make timely policies to prevent the spread of risks across borders. Secondly, the pandemic-induced shocks in international stock markets may last for 6–8 months and come through three phases. In severe phases I) and II) of the epidemic, stock market regulators should strengthen supervision measures, and improve market accountability and transparency. In phase III), the epidemic-induced risks would be incorporated in stock prices, therefore, market supervisors can gradually relax the epidemic response restrictions. Additionally, the results indicate that the epidemic strengthens the spillover contributions of European and American markets but reduces those in most Asian markets. For international investors and portfolio managers, their portfolio risks may be heavily influenced by the pandemic. Thus, they are suggested to hedge the portfolio risks by increasing the investment weights in Asian stock markets and actively managing their portfolios during the pandemic.</section><section><h2>Declaration of interest statement</h2><p>Yuntong Liu, Yu Wei, Qian Wang, and Yi Liu declare no conflicts of interest in this manuscript.</section><section><h2>Acknowledgements</h2><p>The authors are grateful for the financial support from the National Natural Science Foundation of China (71671145, 71971191), Humanities and social science fund of ministry of education of China (17YJA790015, 17XJA790002, 18YJC790132, 18XJA790002), Science and Technology Innovation Team of Yunnan Provincial Universities (2019014) and Yunnan Fundamental Research Projects (202001AS070018).</section><section><h2>Appendix</h2><p><a href="/pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov#fig0002">Fig. A1</a>, <a href="/pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov#fig0003">A2</a>, <a href="/pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov#fig0004">A3</a><figure><h3>Fig. A1.</h3><p><a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/core/lw/2.0/html/tileshop_pmc/tileshop_pmc_inline.html?title=Click%20on%20image%20to%20zoom&amp;p=PMC3&amp;id=8856889_gr2_lrg.jpg"></a><figcaption><p>Dynamic overall spillover based on rolling-window size of 100 days.</figcaption></figure><figure><h3>Fig. A2.</h3><p><a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/core/lw/2.0/html/tileshop_pmc/tileshop_pmc_inline.html?title=Click%20on%20image%20to%20zoom&amp;p=PMC3&amp;id=8856889_gr3_lrg.jpg"></a><figcaption><p>Dynamic overall spillover based on rolling-window size of 130 days.</figcaption></figure><figure><h3>Fig. A3.</h3><p><a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/core/lw/2.0/html/tileshop_pmc/tileshop_pmc_inline.html?title=Click%20on%20image%20to%20zoom&amp;p=PMC3&amp;id=8856889_gr4_lrg.jpg"></a><figcaption><p>Dynamic overall spillover based on rolling-window size of 550 days.</figcaption></figure></section><section><h2>References</h2><section><ol><li><cite>Akhtaruzzaman M., Boubaker S., Sensoy A.
