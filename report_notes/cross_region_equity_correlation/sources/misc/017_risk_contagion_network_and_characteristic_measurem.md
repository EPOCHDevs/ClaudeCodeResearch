critical to understand and prevent contagion between cross-country financial assets. We provide an in-depth analysis of international linkages across markets (stock, bond and foreign exchange), analyzing the dynamics of the linkages in terms of three key levels, namely, returns, volatility and tail risk correlations, as well as an event-based research methodology. For the first time, we propose a multidimensional framework for analyzing the stability of financial networks, based on the consideration of an individual market, asset diversity, and national <a href="https:///topics/economics-econometrics-and-finance/financial-system">financial systems</a><span><span>. The results show that European market in particular highlights the “too connected to fail” phenomenon, which is consistent at the level of returns, volatility and tail correlations. Crisis events or extreme adverse conditions increase the correlation between global financial markets, increasing the intensity and efficiency of contagion between and within developed and emerging markets. The comprehensive impact of the Italian financial market increased particularly during these periods, especially in terms of its linkages with the core European economies. Meanwhile, the results of the simulated attacks show that the <a href="https:///topics/economics-econometrics-and-finance/financial-system">financial systems</a> of countries in Europe and the Americas, such as the UK, France, and Germany, are critical to the stability of the network, especially their stock and bond markets. In particular, the stock market is the most critical to </span><a href="https:///topics/economics-econometrics-and-finance/financial-stability">financial stability</a>. Stock markets, especially in Mexico and South Africa, is the key link between emerging and developed markets.</span></div></div></div><div><h2>Introduction</h2><section><div>In the context of globalization, financial integration is an unstoppable trend (Kose et al., 2009; Broner and Ventura, 2016). Global financial integration can increase linkages between national financial markets and make it easier for investors to diversify their investments across markets, but it also poses challenges. Integration means that shocks in one market can be quickly transmitted to other markets and other countries, and traditional geographic diversification strategies and global portfolio diversification may no longer be as effective as before (Patel et al., 2022; Sewraj et al., 2018). This trend towards interconnectedness may increase the vulnerability of the global financial system, particularly to global crisis events. Examples include the Asian financial crisis in 1997–98, the global financial crisis in 2007–08, and the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020 (Dungey and Martin, 2007; Dias et al., 2019; Altig et al., 2020; Tang and Yao, 2022; Naeem et al., 2023). We aim to help national financial regulators and investors understand the cross-country linkages between different classes of assets in different time periods or under different market conditions, and to help financial regulators formulate more effective policies to improve financial stability based on the results of simulated attacks.</div><div>According to portfolio theory, the extent to which diversification reduces risk depends on the correlation between financial assets in international markets (Levy and Sarnat, 1970). Institutions or investors should invest in international markets that have weak or even insignificant correlations to maximize the benefits of diversification. Conversely, when the interdependence of global financial markets is extremely high, especially in the face of global shocks, volatility in one market can be easily transmitted to other markets or other countries. This may stem from asset linkage effects between national markets. An example is the flow-oriented and stock-oriented models between stock and foreign exchange markets (Dornbusch and Fischer, 1980; Cho et al., 2016; Tian et al., 2023). Again, according to risk preference theory and liquidity preference theory, there is a substitution effect between risky (safe) assets between stock and bond markets (Goyenko and Ukhov, 2009; Baele et al., 2010; Arteaga-Garavito et al., 2024; Brunnermeier et al., 2024). Of course, global macroeconomic factors such as economic growth expectations, inflation, and monetary policy also affect risk contagion among financial markets, and changes in these factors may alter investors' risk appetite and market sentiment (Nguyen and Lambe, 2021; Aleti and Bollerslev, 2024). Problems in some countries have led investors to pay more attention to other countries as well (Jiang et al., 2024; Cole et al., 2025). On this basis, financial market participants may adopt similar trading strategies globally. This “herd behavior” can amplify the risk contagion effect (Forbes and Rigobon, 2002; Yarovaya et al., 2022).</div><div>There is a large body of research assessing cross-country spillovers between financial assets. Ehrmann et al. (2011) find that asset prices are most responsive to other domestic asset price shocks, but there are also large international spillover effects within and across asset classes. Studies of cross-country financial assets typically identify contagion through volatility, correlation, Granger causality, and nonlinear linkage analysis. A large number of studies focus on the cross-country spillover effect between exchange rates and stock, but the findings are not unanimous (Andreou et al., 2013; Cho et al., 2016; Salisu and Ndako, 2018; Tian et al., 2023). This phenomenon may be due to the variability in the selection of country samples, the time span, and the analytical methods used. Therefore, it is still worthwhile to explore this relationship in depth. There are also some studies that focus on the integration of stock, bond, currency, and other markets between countries (regions) (Dees et al., 2007; Ehrmann et al., 2011; Rughoo and You, 2016; Karkowska and Urjasz, 2021). We summarize existing studies and find that stock markets usually receive special attention (Mantegna, 1999; Huang et al., 2009). This is mainly because stock markets are one of the most active and liquid segments of financial markets, and they can quickly reflect market sentiment and macroeconomic changes. Earlier studies extensively analyze cross-country contagion between stock markets during the 2008 global financial crisis (Patel et al., 2022). Studies such as Heil et al. (2022), Belaid et al. (2023), and Naeem et al. (2023) find evidence of contagion among global stock markets during the COVID-19 pandemic. However, while financial integration is a global phenomenon, financial development is not (Mendoza et al., 2009). Financial systems in different countries (regions) differ significantly in terms of their structure, maturity, regulatory environment, and the behavior of market participants. The results of Heil et al. (2022), Belaid et al. (2023) and Naeem et al. (2023) underline that the European stock market plays a key role in the global financial market and has a significant impact on other regions, especially emerging markets.</div><div>The field of spillover effects between financial assets is well established, but we reveal its shortcomings in three key levels to further provide additional perspectives on the dynamic linkages between financial assets, thus enriching the academic discussion in this area. <strong>First</strong>, the limited scope of focus and asset class. Some studies focus mainly on financial markets in developed countries, such as Europe and the United States, and do not pay enough attention to the situation in developing or emerging markets, which may lead to an incomplete understanding of the full picture of global financial integration (Ehrmann et al., 2011; Yarovaya et al., 2022; Naeem et al., 2023). Given the growing influence of emerging markets in the global economy and the increasing openness of their financial markets, our study incorporates emerging markets into the analytical framework to provide a more comprehensive perspective. We analyze not only the correlations among developed markets, but also the relationships between developed and emerging markets, as well as within emerging markets. The results reveal important transmission channels between developed and emerging markets. We also find that some emerging markets are highly sensitive to crisis events and extreme adverse conditions, and that they play an important correlation role in the network. A number of studies use data from specific time periods to examine the impact of a particular event. For example, the Asian financial crisis, the global financial crisis, the US-China trade friction, and the COVID-19 pandemic (Dungey and Martin, 2007; Dias et al., 2019; Shi et al., 2021; Naeem et al., 2023; Bei et al., 2024). We use a wider range of time series data to ensure that the results reflect the correlations of financial markets under different financial risk event shocks. In addition, some studies focus on the stock market and pay insufficient attention to the degree of global financial integration in other asset classes, such as the bond market and the foreign exchange market. Moreover, these studies focus on contagion at the level of returns and volatility (Mantegna, 1999; Huang et al., 2009; Shi et al., 2021; Heil et al., 2022; Zhou and Liu, 2023). We consider different data levels (returns, volatility and tail correlations) and different periods (financial crisis, COVID-19), as well as different tail risk levels (1 %, 10 %, 90 %). Our study extends the analysis to a wider range of asset classes and core metrics of financial asset performance to provide in-depth insights into the role of different asset classes in global financial markets. Our results show that some of the findings in the literature on international stock markets can be generalized to other markets, but most of the results find that stock, bond, and foreign exchange markets differ significantly in terms of risk contagion and impact on the stability of financial networks.</div><div><strong>Second</strong>, there are limits to the scope of tail risk concerns. According to Prospect theory, investors are usually more sensitive to losses than to gains (Kahneman and Tversky, 2013). Therefore, the existing literature focuses on the international contagion of extreme risk in the left tail (Atilgan et al., 2020; Tian et al., 2023). Building on the existing literature, we not only examine left-tail risk levels (1 %, 10 %), but also systematically analyze right-tail risk level (10 %). Our results show that risk contagion in the left (right) tail is significantly heterogeneous. While left-tail risk contagion is important for risk management, right-tail risk contagion should not be ignored. The contagion of right-tail risk may accelerate the formation of asset price bubbles during market booms, and then trigger a chain reaction when the bubble bursts, posing a threat to the stability of the financial system. Therefore, a thorough understanding and effective monitoring of right-tail risk contagion is also crucial for building more robust financial markets and preventing possible financial crises in the future. <strong>Third</strong>, we propose, for the first time, a multidimensional framework for analyzing the stability of financial networks. We simulate and assess the stability of financial networks by examining three dimensions, i.e., the individual market, the diversity of asset classes, and the national financial system, using both random attacks and deliberate attacks. We find that removing the entire financial system of some countries or some of the country's markets significantly slows the speed and efficiency of information flow through the network and weakens the tightness of the financial network's ties. Albert et al. (2000) study the stability of networks and point out the different response mechanisms of scale-free networks in the face of random and deliberate attacks. The network shows extremely high robustness when nodes are randomly removed, but removing highly connected nodes causes the network to split rapidly. Global financial integration may increase risks to financial stability, but assessing and managing the impact on the stability of financial networks following an attack on a country's financial system or its domestic markets may not have received sufficient attention in existing research. Our focus in this area is to provide more in-depth risk assessment and management strategies to ensure the stability of the financial markets in these key countries, which is critical to maintaining the stability of the global financial system.</div><div>To sum up, we adopt a multidimensional perspective to analyze international linkages among different classes of financial assets. To analyze in depth the cross-country linkage effects among stock, bond, and foreign exchange markets, we focus on three key levels of financial metrics: returns, volatility, and tail correlations. Our research not only analyzes the complex linkage effects among these markets, but also provides insights into the influential structures of financial markets and how these structures evolve dynamically in response to changes in the global economic environment. Our study pays particular attention to the importance of individual markets across crisis events (e.g., global financial crisis, COVID-19 pandemic) and across dynamically changing market conditions (e.g., extremely favorable, extremely adverse). We also analyze which countries or asset classes would have a significant impact on the connectivity and functionality of the network following a deliberate attack on each asset class and country's financial system. Our goal is to identify these key financial markets, financial asset classes, and national financial systems so that national regulators can protect against them in their risk management.</div></section></div><div><h2>Section snippets</h2><section><h2>Construction of complex networks</h2><div>We use complex network theory to analyze international risk contagion among different financial asset classes.
