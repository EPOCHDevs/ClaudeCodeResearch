<h3>Data</h3><a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00687-3#Fn1"><span>Footnote </span>1</a><p>This paper selects 15 regions as research samples to examine the relationship between the international stock market and geopolitical risk. The sample includes Russia (ROE), the United Kingdom (UK), France (FRA), Germany (GER), Italy (ITA), China (CN), Hong Kong, Japan (JPA), Korea (KR), India (IND), Indonesia (IDN), the United States (USA), Canada (CAN), Brazil (BRA) and Australia (AU). The main stock indexes of the 15 regions mentioned above are selected for the stock market, and Table&nbsp;<a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00687-3#Tab1">1</a> shows the specific indexes. For geopolitical risk, the overall geopolitical risk (GPR) index constructed by Caldara and Iacoviello (<a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00687-3#ref-CR22">2022</a>) is selected. The total GDP of the research sample accounts for about 80% of global GDP, including both developed and developing economies in regions that have experienced geopolitical events many times since the twenty-first century.</p><div><figure><figcaption><b>Table&nbsp;1 Comparison table of sample area and selected stock index</b></figcaption><div><a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00687-3/tables/1"><span>Full size table</span></a></div></figure></div><a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00687-3#Fn2"><span>Footnote </span>2</a><p>The time interval of the sample selection is from January 2000 to May 2022. Since the twenty-first century, global GPR events have occurred frequently, “black swan” events such as the September 11 terrorist attacks, the Afghanistan war, the Iraq War, the oil crisis, Brexit, and the recent Russian–Ukrainian conflict have seriously threatened to destabilize the international stock market. Furthermore, financial events, such as the bursting of the Internet bubble in 2000, the 2008 financial crisis, the 2010 European sovereign debt crisis, and the 2018 Sino-U.S. trade war have also affected the international order. The sample data are from the Wind database. To reflect the impact of macroeconomic conditions on the global stock market and to describe the risk contagion effect more comprehensively, this paper selects the following four macro control variables based on Härdle et al. (<a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00687-3#ref-CR35">2016</a>) and Xu et al. (<a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00687-3#ref-CR57">2021</a>): (1) the implied volatility index VIX published by the Chicago Board Options Exchange; (2) short term liquidity spreads; (3) yield curve slope; and (4) daily gold futures prices in U.S. dollars. This paper uses data of monthly frequency. To ensure that the stock index returns are at the same frequency as the GPR, monthly data can reduce the noise interference of short term market fluctuations and better reflect the trends and fluctuations of the stock market after being affected by extreme conflicts.</p><h3>The tail risk spillover effect of international stock markets</h3><p>Increasingly frequent geopolitical events in recent years have seriously affected the development of global stock markets. Taking the September 11 attacks as a watershed, the global geopolitical risks and threats after this event have been twice as high as before the event (Caldara and Iacoviello <a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00687-3#ref-CR22">2022</a>). Geopolitical events such as the Iraq War, the 2008 financial crisis, the COVID-19 pandemic, and the recent Russian–Ukrainian conflict have made the current international political and economic landscape even more tense and complex. Thus, this paper first describes the risk contagion characteristics of the international stock market as a whole, concluding that the international stock market’s turbulence is closely related to geopolitical events from the changing trend of the total connectedness of the global stock market. Due to space limitations, the dynamic changes in the total connectedness of international stock markets are shown in Fig.&nbsp;<a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00687-3#Fig12">12</a> of Appendix <a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00687-3#Sec17">A</a>.</p><p>Due to the different degrees of development of the stock markets in the world’s economies, there are great disparities between developed economies and emerging markets; thus, the dynamic effects of import risk and export on stock markets are depicted, respectively, as shown in Figs. <a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00687-3#Fig1">1</a> and <a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00687-3#Fig2">2</a>. Comparing the figures below, the import risk and export in the global stock market have a significant time-varying effect, showing asymmetric characteristics as a whole. At the same time, stock markets of developed economies are more important and influential in the global stock markets, and, therefore, have higher risk spillovers. However, emerging market financial institutions are relatively vulnerable and unable to provide sufficient liquidity for the stock market. Hence, these stock markets cannot effectively resist extreme conflict events, manifesting as high extreme value and significant volatility of import risk (Ahmad et al. <a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00687-3#ref-CR5">2022</a>).</p><div><figure><figcaption><b>Fig.&nbsp;1</b></figcaption><div><div><p>The level of tail risk import in stock markets of various economies</p></div></div><div><a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00687-3/figures/1"><span>Full size image</span></a></div></figure></div><div><figure><figcaption><b>Fig.&nbsp;2</b></figcaption><div><div><p>The level of tail risk export in stock markets of various economies</p></div></div><div><a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00687-3/figures/2"><span>Full size image</span></a></div></figure></div><p>With the outbreak of extreme conflicts, the stock market risk spillover level of developed economies such as the UK and the U.S. has gradually increased, becoming the primary source of risk contagion in the global stock market. Geopolitical conflicts lead to violent fluctuations in investor sentiment, indirectly affecting stock price volatility through the information transmission mechanism and enhancing the risk spillover effect (Benhabib et al. <a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00687-3#ref-CR15">2016</a>). After the outbreak of the oil crisis in 2014, international crude oil market prices fluctuated wildly, affecting the stock markets of oil-exporting economies, such as Indonesia, Russia, and Brazil, and increasing the level of import risk. In 2018, the trade friction between the U.S. and China increased the risk of spillovers in the Chinese stock market. The global COVID-19 pandemic in 2020 has brought the economies of all countries to a standstill, the liquidity of the global financial market has dropped sharply, and the major stock indexes of various economies have plummeted continuously, causing the level of tail risk spillovers in the stock market to rise (Guo et al. <a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00687-3#ref-CR34">2021</a>). In February 2022, after Russia’s military operation against Ukraine, the U.S. imposed a series of economic sanctions on Russia, such as SWIFT sanctions, SDN list sanctions, etc., to force it to stop military operations. These sanctions have accelerated the cross-border contagion of tail risks, manifested by a sharp rise in Russian import risk. However, with China’s “dual circulation” strategy in early 2020, the domestic demand for China’s economic growth has been continuously released, enhancing the ability to resist external risks. In addition, China has effectively resisted the impact of COVID-19 and has not caused significant volatility in the stock market and investor panic. Hence, the level of import risk showed a gradual downward trend. Even after the outbreak of the Russia–Ukraine conflict, the uptick in tail import risk did not show extreme volatility.</p><h3>Tail risk network of international stock markets</h3><p>After analyzing the tail risk level of the global stock market from the time dimension, this paper examines the tail risk connectedness of the international stock market from the spatial dimension. Taking the world’s major economies as complex network nodes, the tail risk connectedness network between economies is constructed, with the thickness of the edge representing the direction and magnitude of risk transmission in the network layer. The average value of the risk spillover level is selected as the threshold to simplify the network, i.e., the edges that are smaller than the average value in the risk connectedness matrix are removed, and the tail risk spillover network is obtained, as shown in Fig.&nbsp;<a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00687-3#Fig3">3</a>.</p><div><figure><figcaption><b>Fig.&nbsp;3</b></figcaption><div><div><p>The tail risk spillover network of international stock markets</p></div></div><div><a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00687-3/figures/3"><span>Full size image</span></a></div></figure></div><p>Despite global stock markets being strongly correlated overall, there is heterogeneity in the tail risk contagion among different economies. The economies of Western Europe, the U.S., and Canada have higher levels of risk spillovers, indicating that stock markets of developed economies are more prone to contagion of financial tail risks, and have a significant impact on global stock markets (Baumohl and Shahzad <a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00687-3#ref-CR12">2019</a>). China, India, and Brazil have a relatively high degree of import risk in the stock market. On the one hand, financial markets in emerging economies are less responsive, making them more vulnerable to external risks when extreme events occur (Lai et al. <a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00687-3#ref-CR44">2023</a>). Conversely, complex and volatile geopolitical factors (for example, the India–Pakistan conflict) increase the vulnerability of the relevant economies’ stock markets and expose them to greater transnational financial risk contagion.</p><p>To further analyze the risk contagion law of the international stock market under the influence of geopolitical conflicts, this paper selects four time nodes of the September 11 incident, the 2008 financial crisis, the Sino-U.S. trade friction and the Russia–Ukraine conflict to conduct partial risk contagion analysis in the network. Limited by space, the tail risk spillover networks of the stock market during the 9/11 incident and the 2018 Sino-U.S. trade friction period are shown in Figs. <a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00687-3#Fig13">13</a> and <a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00687-3#Fig14">14</a> in Appendix <a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00687-3#Sec17">A</a>. Geopolitical conflict events are also closely related to the tail risk contagion of international stock markets.</p><p>During the international financial crisis, the overdue repayment of many subprime mortgages caused market panic, depositors withdrew cash collectively, and bank liquidity quickly decreased (Bernanke <a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00687-3#ref-CR17">2018</a>). At the same time, the sharp fall in asset prices detracted from bank capital, and limited leverage inhibited bank credit creation, leading to the rapid spread of systemic risk (Baron et al. <a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00687-3#ref-CR11">2021</a>). Figure&nbsp;<a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00687-3#Fig4">4</a> shows that during the 2008 financial crisis, as the main sources of risk contagion in the tail risk spillover network, economies such as the U.S. and Europe played the role of risk contagion. Generally, the connectedness between nodes is strong, indicating that the financial crisis has spread widely, and tail risks are transmitted from developed economies to emerging markets. The Russian–Ukrainian conflict outbreak in 2022 has further aggravated global turbulence, affecting price fluctuations of energy, metals, food and other commodities and increasing global economic uncertainty, causing a sharp increase in the overall risk level of the global stock market. Figure&nbsp;<a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00687-3#Fig5">5</a> shows that Russia has a relatively high level of import risk and is the primary risk receiver in this event. Its risk sources are mainly European economies. This shows that NATO economies have been greatly affected by this conflict, and the frequent introduction of economic policies by the U.S. and the cross-border flow of a large amount of capital have caused their stock prices to fluctuate violently, resulting in tail risk spillovers. In addition, the Moscow Stock Exchange stopped trading for a month since February 26, causing stock price volatility to stagnate, thus showing a low degree of tail risk spillover in the network.</p><div><figure><figcaption><b>Fig.&nbsp;4</b></figcaption><div><div><p>The tail risk spillover network during the 2008 financial crisis</p></div></div><div><a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00687-3/figures/4"><span>Full size image</span></a></div></figure></div><div><figure><figcaption><b>Fig.&nbsp;5</b></figcaption><div><div><p>The tail risk spillover network during the Russia-Ukraine conflict in 2022</p></div></div><div><a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00687-3/figures/5"><span>Full size image</span></a></div></figure></div><p>The risk contagion analysis above illustrates that geopolitical events intensify the international stock market risk contagion. The rise of GPR exacerbates the information asymmetry problem in the stock markets, leading to a decline in stock liquidity and increased uncertainty in the market (Fiorillo et al. <a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00687-3#ref-CR30">2023</a>). Meanwhile, the sharp fluctuations in stock prices during the financial crisis will also increase the probability of extreme conflict events. The above-mentioned risk contagion dynamic represents information contagion in the risk contagion mechanism. Increased GPR drives the herd and convergence effects, intensifying stock price fluctuations and risk contagion. Since the 2008 financial crisis, the financial regulators of various economies have taken many measures to deal with the growing market risks. However, external risk shocks negatively impact stock market stability in multiple countries. With the frequent occurrence of geopolitical events, it has become increasingly important to guard against the import of external risks.</p><h3>The connectedness network of geopolitical risk</h3><p>Frequent extreme conflict events have led to the steady deterioration of international geopolitical relations, eroding the stability of the global stock market and accelerating the accumulation of systemic risks. Therefore, in this section, the above 15 economies are used as network nodes, and the Granger causality of the GPR index of each economy is used as the edge to describe the connectedness network of global GPR. Based on the full sample data, the GPR index during the Russian–Ukrainian conflict period is excluded to reflect the time-varying characteristics of global GPR contagion.</p><p>Figure&nbsp;<a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00687-3#Fig6">6</a> shows that the U.S., Canada, and Germany are the main exporters of GPRs; however, there are significant differences in the export directions, implying that global GPRs often stem from local disputes. At the same time, Australia, Brazil and Italy are the main importers of risks, which are infected by GPRs in nearly half of the sample economies. Australia and Brazil’s unique geographical location and rich mineral resources make them the focus of the game between the world’s major economies. As competition in the Pacific and conflicts in South America intensify, so do the GPRs. After excluding data from the Russia–Ukraine conflict period, Fig.&nbsp;<a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00687-3#Fig7">7</a> shows that the node out-degree of each European economy and the overall network connectedness have significantly decreased. This demonstrates that the GPRs caused by the Russia–Ukraine conflict have a wide range of impacts and are highly contagious between regions. However, the GPR contagion in the U.S. and Canada has not changed much, indicating that their risk spillover effects are stable and at the network’s core. Also, during the crisis period (Fig.&nbsp;<a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00687-3#Fig6">6</a> compared to Fig.&nbsp;<a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00687-3#Fig7">7</a>), the BRICS economies are more closely linked and have strong connectedness, indicating that emerging economies can work together to mitigate the economic impact of external risk input. As China’s President Xi Jinping said at the opening ceremony of the BRICS Foreign Ministers’ Meeting on May, 2022, it is necessary to strengthen political mutual trust and security cooperation, and jointly build a community of security for humankind.</p><div><figure><figcaption><b>Fig.&nbsp;6</b></figcaption><div><div><p>Geographical network during the full sample period</p></div></div><div><a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00687-3/figures/6"><span>Full size image</span></a></div></figure></div><div><figure><figcaption><b>Fig.&nbsp;7</b></figcaption><div><div><p>Geographical network after excluding the period of Russia-Ukraine conflict</p></div></div><div><a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00687-3/figures/7"><span>Full size image</span></a></div></figure></div><p>According to the above analysis, local geopolitical conflicts, if uncontrolled, can easily cause global turmoil, leading to stock market turmoil. By comparing the tail risk network of stock market returns with the GPR connectedness network, the stock market often produces tail risk contagion when extreme risk events occur. Therefore, risks are contagious within a single network and interact between different network layers. If risk contagion analysis is only performed within the layer, much key information from other network layers will be ignored, affecting the systemic risk measurement of the overall network. Thus, this paper studies the risk contagion mechanism between economies from the perspective of a two-layer complex network of interactive contagion between international stock markets and GPRs, providing a new perspective for accurately measuring systemic risks among global economies.</p><h3>The two-layer network of international stock markets and geopolitical risks</h3><p>GPRs are closely related to international stock markets and have complex interaction paths. As a barometer of macroeconomic conditions, the stock market can first reflect the running trend of the economic cycle. At the same time, the surge of international floating capital and the pursuit of safe-haven assets caused by geopolitical conflicts make stock prices fluctuate violently (Benhabib et al. <a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00687-3#ref-CR15">2016</a>). In addition, the frequent outbreak of local geopolitical conflicts will eventually change the global political pattern, affecting normal international trade and causing imbalances in the balance sheets of import and export companies in various countries. At the same time, the high volatility of the stock market will make the policy uncertainty of the economy remain high, and then affect the return volatility with its trading partners (Boutchkova et al. <a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00687-3#ref-CR20">2012</a>). Hence, this section will construct a two-layer network analysis framework of international stock markets and GPRs, and study the complex interaction effects between the two from the perspective of spatial connectedness.</p><p>The world’s major economies are used as nodes in the two-layer network of international stock markets and geopolitics. Figure&nbsp;<a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00687-3#Fig8">8</a> shows that the risk contagion between international stock markets and GPRs is used to characterize the edges of the two-layer network. The upper layer slice network represents the interconnectedness of GPR contagion among economies, while the lower-layer network represents the risk spillover of stock price fluctuations between economies. Risk contagion exists within the individual network and between network layers. Each node’s color depth and size reflect the weight of the eigenvector centrality index in the two-layer network. The darker the color, the stronger the centrality in the network.</p><div><figure><figcaption><b>Fig.&nbsp;8</b></figcaption><div><div><p>Two-layer network of international stock markets and geopolitics</p></div></div><div><a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00687-3/figures/8"><span>Full size image</span></a></div></figure></div><p>As can be seen from the figure, risk contagion among economies exists within each network layer and transmits across the layers between two-layer networks through various information transmission mechanisms. Thus, merely considering stock market volatility or GPR misses much effective information, weakening the connectedness of economies in the two-layer network and eventually underestimating systemic risks, leading to a global financial crisis. The cross-layer contagion between the international stock market and the geopolitical double-layer network indicates that the cross-risk contagion between GPR and stock market volatility cannot be ignored.</p><p>In the international stock market–geopolitics double-layer network, the total risk connectedness within the geopolitical network layer and the stock price fluctuation layer are 949.12 and 918.3, respectively. The total interlayer risk connectedness transmitted from the geopolitical network layer to the stock price fluctuation layer is 440.64. The total interlayer risk connectedness transmitted from the stock price fluctuation layer to the geopolitical network layer is 336.26. The above connectedness shows that the risk spillover effect within the geopolitical network layer is significantly higher than that of the stock price fluctuation network layer, and the risk spillover from the GPR layer to the stock price fluctuation layer is more substantial. On the whole, GPR, as an external shock, seriously affects relevant economies’ investment activities and economic stability. It exacerbates the price fluctuations of various financial derivatives, unbalanced financial institutions’ balance sheets in the risk contagion mechanism, and fails the payment and clearing infrastructures, thereby accelerating the risk contagion of international stock markets (Abakah et al. <a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00687-3#ref-CR2">2022</a>). In addition, in the two-layer network, the importance ranking of most nodes is roughly the same, and the U.S., France, and Italy all show systemic importance in the network of stock market volatility and GPR.</p><p>Through the two-layer network analysis of international stock markets and geopolitics, it can be found that there is a complex interaction between the stock market and the geopolitical double-layer network. To deeply analyze the characteristics of the two-layer network of international stock markets and geopolitics, indicators such as strength centrality, eigenvector centrality, and authority centrality are selected to investigate the systemically important economies of the two-layer network.</p><p>Table <a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00687-3#Tab2">2</a> shows that the centrality metrics of the U.S. and European economies are ranked consistently at different network layers, occupying the core position. For historical reasons, geopolitical events such as the Northern Ireland issue, the French-Turkish conflict, and the French-German conflict have a greater impact on the connectedness of the stock market and geopolitics. Since the beginning of the twenty-first century, the U.S. has been particularly active in the global geopolitical arena, including the wars in Afghanistan, Libya, and Syria as well as the recent conflict between Russia and Ukraine. At the same time, the stock markets of developed economies play a pivotal role in international stock markets. To maintain their own economic stability, risks will spread to developing economies. Additionally, India has a stronger systemic importance in the geopolitical risk layer. The friction between India and its neighboring economies has deepened in recent years, and territorial disputes have become increasingly prominent. The repeated outbreaks of localized fire conflicts have made India’s geopolitical risk surge; thus, its centrality geopolitical risk indicator is high.</p><div><figure><figcaption><b>Table&nbsp;2 The centrality of the international stock market and geopolitics two-tier network</b></figcaption><div><a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00687-3/tables/2"><span>Full size table</span></a></div></figure></div><p>The comprehensive examination of the changes of systemically important economies using strength, eigenvector and authority indexes indicates that the centrality index rankings of some economies are not significantly different in the same attribute network layer, although there are great differences in different network layers. For example, India ranks in the top three in the geopolitical risk layer but well below the geopolitical risk layer in other network layers indicators. This shows that each economy’s centrality ranking is affected by its own situation and the attributes of the network where it is located. Some deviation based on different centrality measurement indexes will occur. Therefore, when examining the node characteristics of the network, the measurement system of node centrality should be improved to reduce the error as much as possible. Comparing the aggregation and the two-layer networks shows that the centrality rankings of China, Japan, South Korea, and other economies have risen significantly because the aggregation network formed by coupling the stock price volatility layer and the geopolitical risk layer lose some important information. Thus, the two-layer network should be preferred when studying different network characteristics, which is more representative.</p><h3>The two-layer network of international stock markets and geopolitical risks</h3><p>After clarifying the complex connectedness between the international stock market and geopolitical risk, to quantify the interlayer risk contagion effect and intralayer risk spillover effect between the two-layer network of international stock markets and the geopolitical risks, Table&nbsp;<a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00687-3#Tab3">3</a> calculates the risk spillover matrix of the double-layer network. The main diagonal elements of the risk spillover matrix reflect the influence of the node’s own lag effect in the network layer with the same attributes (Antonakakis et al. <a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00687-3#ref-CR7">2018</a>). The From column and the To row reflect the import and export risk effects, respectively, the NET row represents the net spillover effect, the shaded part is the risk spillover index within the layer, and the blank part represents the interaction of the risk between layers.</p><div><figure><figcaption><b>Table&nbsp;3 Risk spillover matrix of international stock market and geopolitics two-tier network</b></figcaption><div><a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00687-3/tables/3"><span>Full size table</span></a></div></figure></div><p>Comparing the main diagonal elements of the risk spillover matrix illustrates that the influence of the lag effect of different economies in the geopolitical risk layer on the current period is slightly higher than that in the stock price volatility layer, indicating that the lag information of geopolitical risk has a more pronounced effect on itself in the short term. However, the stock market fluctuations of various economies are often affected by medium and long-term factors such as macro policies and economic cycles; thus, in the short term, their lagging information has less impact on the current period. Simultaneously, India, Indonesia, and Brazil have a higher proportion of lagged information in the geopolitical risk layer, while their external risk spillover values are lower. This shows that some emerging markets do not have a sufficient voice on the world stage, their own policy spillover effects and risk diffusion capabilities are weak, and they cannot effectively disperse geopolitical risks.</p><p>From the perspective of export risk, the spillover index of the UK and the U.S. ranked in the top two in the stock price volatility layer, with 119.09 and 108.59, respectively. The spillover index of China also ranked relatively high, with 94.43 in the fifth place. However, Russia, Canada, and Australia showed lower spillover levels of 51.16, 57.71 and 65.03 respectively. Economies such as the UK and the U.S. have relatively mature stock market structures and mature systems due to the relatively early development of the financial industry. The market value of its listed companies accounts for 60% of the total market value of listed companies worldwide. It occupies the dominant position in the global stock market and acts as the bellwether that affects global stock market volatility (Korkusuz et al. <a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00687-3#ref-CR43">2023</a>). Since China’s market-oriented reform in 1998, although the stock market has developed rapidly, it still has the characteristics of imperfect market structure and poor ability to deal with risks, such as the A-share stock market crash in 2015. In addition, in recent years, extreme events such as Asia–Pacific regional friction, the Sino-U.S. trade conflict, and the COVID-19 pandemic have had a significant impact on China’s stock market, which has led to the accumulation of systemic risks in the market and made China’s risk spillover index high.</p><p>The U.S. is the main export risker in the GPR layer, and its GPR spillover index is as high as 135.71. A large gap exists between the “second echelon” economies such as China and the UK. The U.S. has played a pivotal role in international geopolitical events in recent years. But at the same time, the global geopolitical landscape is slowly changing. The table shows that the GPR spillover index of economies such as China and the UK is about 124, indicating that China is gradually playing a more important role in the geopolitical pattern. Russia’s GPR spillover index is 104.95, which is above the average value. This is due to the constant frictions in Eastern Europe, the intensified conflict between Russia and Ukraine, and the gradual expansion of NATO, which has made the geopolitical situation tense again. Moreover, the risk spillover level of countries in the GPR layer is slightly higher than that in the stock price volatility layer. This is due to the frequent occurrence of geopolitical events in recent years. With the deepening of global economic integration, the spillover effect of GPRs on the stock market has increased.</p><p>From the perspective of import risk, the import index in regions with a higher level of capital account openness, such as Australia and the U.S., is higher in the stock price volatility layer. However, China’s import index, which is the leading exporter of stock price volatility risk, is relatively low, indicating that the openness of the capital market will directly affect the level of risk contagion between economies. Hence, the slow liberalization of the capital account in developing economies can maintain the stability of financial markets to a certain extent. China and Australia are the main risk receivers in the GPR layer. With China’s peaceful rise, the Sino-U.S. trade friction reflects a conflict of interest between developed economies and emerging markets. Factors such as trade disputes and core technology monopoly have increased the GPRs China faces. Unlike the export index, the import index in the two-layer network layer is relatively similar, indicating that the export risk of different economies is quite different, while the differences in import risk are small.</p><p>Combined with the net spillover level, the U.S.’s GPR net spillover index is 51.66, acting as the main risk net exporter between single-layer and double-layer networks. From the results in the table, the U.S. is the core of the global stock market and geopolitical connectedness network, and it has a systemically important position in both the international stock market and the geopolitical arena. In general, the net spillover index of the geopolitical risk layer is slightly larger than that of the stock price volatility layer, indicating that the risk contagion effect of geopolitical risk to the stock market is more significant, i.e., the emergence of geopolitical crisis events will trigger turbulence in the international stock market (Fiorillo et al. <a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00687-3#ref-CR30">2023</a>).</p><h3>Dynamic network of international stock markets and geopolitics</h3><p>To explore the time-varying trend of the connectedness between international stock markets and geopolitics, this section selects two-layer network topology indicators (Authority centrality, Katz centrality, and StrengthOut centrality) to reflect its dynamic change process. Authority centrality is based on the centrality of eigenvectors, and the “Authority” index is selected to measure its importance in considering the overall connectedness of the global stock market and individual extreme conditions. Therefore, this paper uses it to describe the changing trend of connectedness between stock markets and geopolitics in the whole sample period. StrengthOut centrality directly reflects the influence of a node in the network, while Katz centrality depends more on the importance of its adjacent nodes. Therefore, the dynamic change index before and after the September 11 incident is selected as Katz centrality, which more comprehensively reflects the impact of the Internet bubble on global technology companies. During the Russian–Ukrainian conflict, the StrengthOut centrality is selected to analyze the systemic importance characteristics of relevant economies.</p><p>In the total sample period, the five permanent member economies of the United Nations are selected to reflect the dynamic changes of the cross-risk between international stock markets and geopolitics. Figure&nbsp;<a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00687-3#Fig9">9</a> shows the network connectedness indicators of various economies fluctuated to varying degrees before and after September 11, during the 2008 international financial crisis, during the COVID-19 pandemic, and after the outbreak of the Russian–Ukrainian conflict in 2022. The September 11 incident set off a wave of international antiterrorist operations. The U.S.-UK coalition forces and NATO countries immediately launched military strikes against the “Al Qaeda” organization in Afghanistan and the Iraqi Saddam regime, which led to the turbulent economic and political situation in the Middle East, which in turn caused the level of cross-risk contagion between international stock markets and geopolitics increased sharply. After the outbreak of COVID-19 in 2020, countries worldwide have stopped work and production. Manufacturing and service industry stagnation has led to a cliff-like decline in economic levels, and the cross risks between international stock markets and geopolitics have risen again. Since 2022, COVID-19 has continued to spread, the game between significant powers has continued to escalate, and the geopolitical situation has been unpredictable. The above factors have led to violent fluctuations in cross-risk contagion between international stock markets and geopolitics. However, during the 2008 international financial crisis and the 2010 European debt crisis, the volatility of the cross-risk level of various countries was small, indicating that extreme events, such as military conflict and public health emergencies, have become important factors for the cross-risk contagion between the stock market and geopolitics.</p><div><figure><figcaption><b>Fig.&nbsp;9</b></figcaption><div><div><p>Dynamic network of international stock markets and geopolitics</p></div></div><div><a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00687-3/figures/9"><span>Full size image</span></a></div></figure></div><p>Since the cross-risk contagion volatility between the stock market and geopolitics during the September 11 event and the Russian–Ukrainian conflict in 2022 is more complicated, this paper will further analyze the cross-risk contagion at the above two time points, as shown in Figs. <a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00687-3#Fig10">10</a> and <a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00687-3#Fig11">11</a>. The reason for the selection is that the Internet bubble event mainly affected technology and Internet companies in developed economies, and the GDP levels of the economies mentioned above were at the forefront during the Internet bubble period. In addition, NATO countries such as the U.S. and the UK are the main participants in the war in Afghanistan, and Japan has provided logistical support for NATO. In analyzing the level of cross-risk contagion in the period of the 2022 Russian–Ukrainian conflict, the U.S., Russia, the UK, Germany, and China were selected for analysis.</p><div><figure><figcaption><b>Fig.&nbsp;10</b></figcaption><div><div><p>Dynamic network of international stock markets and geopolitics (during the 9.11 event)</p></div></div><div><a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00687-3/figures/10"><span>Full size image</span></a></div></figure></div><div><figure><figcaption><b>Fig.&nbsp;11</b></figcaption><div><div><p>Dynamic network of international stock markets and geopolitics (during the Russian-Ukrainian conflict)</p></div></div><div><a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00687-3/figures/11"><span>Full size image</span></a></div></figure></div><p>Before the dotcom bubble, companies on the Nasdaq had an overall price-to-earnings ratio as high as 200, more than triple the price-to-earnings ratio when Japan’s housing bubble burst in 1991. The continuous decline over the past two years has severely damaged the vitality of American Internet companies. As shown in Fig.&nbsp;<a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-024-00687-3#Fig10">10</a>, the level of cross-risk contagion in the U.S. remained high during this period, while the level of cross-risk contagion in economies such as Britain and France gradually declined. Until the 9/11 incident in 2001, global panic spread, and geopolitical risks became a major problem that affected the stable development of the world economy. The level of cross-risk contagion between stock markets and geopolitics across economies, reflected in the graph, has risen sharply to historic highs. In February 2022, Putin announced a special military operation in the Donbas region, marking the full outbreak of the Russian–Ukrainian conflict. Subsequently, the levels of cross-risk contagion between stock markets and geopolitics in economies such as the U.S. and the UK have risen, indicating that the conflict has a wide-ranging impact. However, the overall level of cross-risk in China is on a downward trend, indicating that China has successfully resisted the input of external risks in this conflict and did not cause drastic fluctuations in the stock market. The above analysis shows that major geopolitical events will affect the level of tail risk contagion in the international stock market through the connectedness between stock markets and geopolitics. However, risk contagion is more concentrated between event-related regions due to the localized characteristics of geographical events.</p>
