We find that the level, slope, and curvature of the VIX's term structure are significant predictors of future returns. Decomposing the VIX of individual stocks into physical volatility and variance risk premiums reveals that the variance risk premium of individual stocks is a significant predictor of future returns. Finally, the VIX and variance risk premiums of individual stocks are priced differently for different time horizons.</div></div></div></div></div><div><div><h2>Introduction</h2><section><div>In 1993, the Chicago Board Options Exchange (CBOE) introduced the Volatility Index (VIX), which has become the key benchmark of expected short-term market volatility, and an index on whose basis futures and options contracts on volatility could be written. A growing number of studies have examined the market's VIX and confirmed that this index contains information that is useful in predicting future market activity (Banerjee&nbsp;et&nbsp;al., 2007; Wang&nbsp;and Yen,&nbsp;2018; Ding&nbsp;et&nbsp;al., 2021; Bansal&nbsp;et&nbsp;al., 2022).</div><div>While the vast majority of the research studies the VIX of the S&amp;P 500, few, if any, works have investigated the VIX of individual stocks over the next 1–12 months. Thus, our goal was to fill this major gap in the literature by calculating the VIX of individual stocks and examining its information content. This is a relatively unexplored topic and one that has been previously overlooked in the literature.</div><div>Our study contributes to the existing literature in several ways. First, we construct a VIX-style measure of individual stocks over the next 1, 2, 3, 6, 9, and 12 months. Such VIX data are not provided by data vendors, and hence, can be viewed as private information not available to the public. We extend the literature by exploring the effect of the whole shape of the VIX curve of individual stocks (i.e., their level, slope and curvature) on the future evolution of stock prices. More specifically, we analyze the information implied in the whole shape of the VIX's term structure curve by utilizing the latent factors model of Nelson&nbsp;and Siegel&nbsp;(1987).</div><div>Earlier studies have calculated the term structure of the S&amp;P 500′s VIX. They reported that its slope reflects information about the price of variance risk (Johnson,&nbsp;2017), and that valuable information embedded in it improves options pricing (Cao&nbsp;et&nbsp;al., 2020). However, to the best of our knowledge, no prior study has attempted to evaluate the information embedded in the VIX's term structure of individual stocks or how its shape, in terms of its level, slope and curvature, reflects information about future evolutions in the assets’ prices.</div><div>Second, we add to the literature dealing with the relationship between risk and return by decomposing the VIX of individual stocks into two components: the variance risk premium (VRP) and physical variance. Given that the variance in the returns of financial securities is stochastic and time-varying, investors usually want to prevent such variability. Therefore, they are willing to pay a premium to protect themselves from variances in risk. Theoretically, the idea that the uncertainty surrounding the variance in an asset's returns commands a compensation premium relies on the nonstandard recursive utility framework of Epstein&nbsp;and Zin&nbsp;(1991) as well as Weil&nbsp;(1989). Both studies assume that agents prefer the early resolution of uncertainty and, hence, should be willing to pay more for insurance against volatility shocks.</div><div>Building on these foundations, we conduct an in-depth analysis and examine the predictive content of the variance risk premium at the individual security level. Earlier studies dealing with aggregate market returns report that the variance risk premium can predict aggregate stock returns in the short run (Bollerslev&nbsp;et&nbsp;al., 2009, 2011). Some studies have documented that the term structure of the variance risk premium slopes downward (Aït-Sahalia&nbsp;et&nbsp;al., 2020). On the other hand, Li&nbsp;and Zinna&nbsp;(2018) demonstrate that it slopes upwards. Given this discrepancy, we extend the literature and investigate the behavior of the variance risk of individual stocks.</div><div>The first important finding indicates that individual VIX indices exhibit a modest correlation with the aggregate VIX of the S&amp;P 500, suggesting that much of the variation in individual stocks is firm specific. This finding holds true regardless of the maturity term.</div><div>The second finding indicates that the VIX term structure of the individual stocks slopes upwards. The rise in VIX levels across various maturity periods for each stock could suggest that market participants anticipate volatility, potentially leading to fluctuations in individual stock prices. Fig.&nbsp;1 displays this key finding.</div><div>Our third finding suggests that the variance risk premium is a promising indicator of where the security's price is heading in the short run. Finally, given that the variance risk premium furnishes valuable information, we decompose its term structure and observe distinct pricing patterns for longer time horizons (see Fig.&nbsp;2). This result hints that investors seek greater compensation for the uncertainty surrounding the variance in returns in the distant future. This result also accords with the fact that market participants and academics regard the variance risk premium as an indicator of risk aversion (e.g., Bekaert&nbsp;and Hoerova,&nbsp;2014; Qadan&nbsp;and Shuval,&nbsp;2022).</div><div>This study adds to the literature about constructing financial predictors based on information extracted from equity options (e.g., Han&nbsp;and Li,&nbsp;2021). The outcome of this study should provide a variety of applications that can help market participants protect their portfolios and improve their yields. Understanding the VIX's term structure as well as the term structure of the variance risk premium of individual stocks helps create volatility derivatives and products in exchanges quite rapidly, which is crucial for pricing volatility derivatives and constructing hedging trading strategies. This capability is particularly significant given the growing attention to volatility products and the need to hedge against uncertainty.</div><div>This study proceeds as follows.
