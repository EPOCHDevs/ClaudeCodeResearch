<div><div><h2>Introduction</h2><section><p>This study investigates the pattern of spillover and connectedness between a broad set of financial assets (equities, commodities, bonds, and VIX) and its implications for portfolio diversification strategies. In recent years, increased interest in international portfolio diversification has motivated investors to search for assets and markets that can provide a cushion against shocks during periods of financial turmoil. Thus, cross-market or cross-asset linkages have become an important topic, both for academics and investors, especially with the increasing correlation between commodities and equity markets after the global financial crisis.<sup>1</sup><p>A key element of the argument in the literature is that to achieve higher portfolio diversification benefits, it is imperative to understand the co-movements, interdependence, and spillover among various assets (markets). Logically, for practical investment purposes, market participants are expected to be more concerned about the magnitude and directions of net return spillover contributions by different asset classes in their portfolios. Consistent with such realistic investment expectations, a large body of growing literature examines the total and net directional spillover effects among different markets (assets) using the intuitive spillover approach of Diebold and Yilmaz, 2009, Diebold and Yilmaz, 2012. The existing spillover literature in cross-market or cross-asset settings predominantly examines the presence of spillover, its direction, and the size of spillover shocks.<sup>2</sup> Available empirical evidence in the existing literature is subject to three shortcomings.<p>First, one important issue that requires further information is the way the information on spillovers can be used to enhance hedging effectiveness in portfolio management activities. The available literature concludes that market participants can achieve better portfolio diversification benefits by incorporating the size and direction of the net return spillover information. However, there is insufficient evidence on the empirical assessment of such information (Mensi et al. 2018; Yoon et al. 2018). To overcome these return spillover shocks, whether a long or short position on a particular asset can generate a higher return without increasing the portfolio risk is an important empirical question. Realistically, the continuous risk-on or risk-off trading strategies adjust the overall portfolio risk with the cross-assets' upward or downward valuations, and eventually, the returns of these assets tend to move together. In an environment with constant investment opportunities, an increase in cross-country return correlation unambiguously reduces the benefits of international portfolio diversification and optimal intertemporal global portfolio choices (Antonakakis et al. 2018; Arouri et al. 2011; Basher and Sadorsky 2016).<sup>3</sup> On the contrary, in a bipolar market environment, where correlations among assets are increasingly high, almost all asset prices go up when “risk is on,” and all asset prices go down when “risk is off” Given the increasing correlation, a new piece of information in terms of the spillover effect (transmitter or receiver of shock) among assets will have no tangible benefit for the investors, unless the long (buy) or short (sell) investment positions are calibrated in terms of quantifiable outcomes.<p>Second, the existing empirical literature on spillover and connectedness has provided limited assessment of portfolios comprised of either large numbers or varieties of the assets available to investors to achieve portfolio diversification. Apart from equities, the majority of studies only focus on two commodities (oil and gold) (Kang and Lee 2019; Khalfaoui et al. 2015; Mensi et al. 2018; Yoon et al. 2018; Zhang 2017) and barring a few exceptions (e.g., Ciner et al. 2018) minimal attention has been given to the benefit of other commodities, specifically metal and agricultural commodities, for portfolio diversification. There is also limited focus on the spillover phenomena combining bond and volatility indices with equity and commodity markets. This is of paramount concern given the importance of cross-market (cross-asset) linkage for the effectiveness of hedging strategies with a multi-asset portfolio. Another methodological issue that arises with limited asset spillover analysis is related to the total spillover index calculation through Diebold and Yilmaz, 2009, Diebold and Yilmaz, 2012. The total spillover value in a system of assets can range from zero to one; thus, the total spillover index's proximity to a unit value greatly influences the net directional spillover assessment of other assets. Given that the direction and intensity of network connectedness are sensitive to the choice of financial assets and economic events, a large set of the investable asset classes can provide a more meaningful interpretation of results. Additionally, as global investors persistently pursue attractive asset classes to allocate their portfolios with an alternative style of investing (Syriopoulos et al. 2015) there is scope to revisit the spillover and connectedness analysis with sizeable diversifiable test assets. Indeed, there is limited empirical scrutiny of financial market data on the spillover and connectedness issue in the post-crisis period. The post-crisis sample period has many important implications for investment managers, as it includes several prominent global events.<sup>4</sup><p>Third, empirical evidence related to cross-assets' or cross-markets' co-movement encompassing spillover effects through Diebold and Yilmaz, 2009, Diebold and Yilmaz, 2012 and wavelet-based time-frequency scale is undeniably scant in the related literature.<sup>5</sup> The Diebold-Yilmaz model focuses primarily on the time domain spillover effect. However, it omits the important aspect of shock transmission across frequencies due to the diverse investment horizon (or frequency) preferences of market participants. The heterogeneity created by varied expectations of market participants concerning investment horizon or trading frequencies may produce divergent responses to the same information shock that appears in the market. Wavelet-based time-frequency analysis is a powerful mathematical tool for time-series analysis (Bodart and Candelon 2009; Ciner et al. 2018; Genҫay et al. 2002; Gallegati 2012).<sup>6</sup> It provides a time-frequency representation and helps clarify the frequency contributions of a time series at any point in time. Wavelet applications using time-frequency analysis helps to distinguish between different relations that may be time-invariant but frequency variant. Taken together, the use of a wavelet-based approach, along with a spillover approach, can help us determine whether the increase in cross-market linkages is due to long-term (low frequency) or short-term (high frequency) components.<p>Given these noticeable gaps in the related literature, this study, using daily data of 18 stock markets, and ten other assets (bonds, commodities, and VIX indices), extends three lines of empirical inquiry.<sup>7</sup> First, we examine the direction of spillover and the extent of connectedness between different asset classes using Diebold and Yilmaz, 2009, Diebold and Yilmaz, 2012’s spillover approach. Apart from suggesting the strength of spillover or connectedness between assets, the network approach also sheds light on the behavior of assets and markets as to whether they are a recipient (transmitter) of shocks from (to) others. The second line of inquiry into the development of dynamic spillover and connectedness is provided from the time-frequency perspective using wavelet multiple correlation and cross-correlation following the approach of Fernández-Macho, 2012, Fernández-Macho, 2018. The wavelet multiple correlation and cross-correlation aim to measure the overall statistical relationships that might exist at different time scales or frequencies among a set of observations on a multivariate random variable (Fernández-Macho 2012). Our final assessment examines whether more specific information, like spillover effects and connectedness based classification of assets as shock-transmitter or shock-receiver, can help achieve better hedging effectiveness without minimizing the return. Using the standard long-short portfolio construction approach (Antonakakis et al. 2018; Sadorsky 2014; Syriopoulos et al. 2015), we explore whether spillover-based information transmission (shock-receiver or shock-transmitter) can help design an effective asset allocation and portfolio diversification strategy.<p>Our findings can be briefly summarized as follows. Results reveal that most of the emerging markets (EMs) are the net receivers of shocks, whereas most of the developed markets are transmitters of shocks. The equity markets appear to be shock transmitters to the commodities (such as oil, metals, gold, and energy) and bonds. Among the developed markets, the US stock market is a major shock transmitter to the EMs. We also note that bonds are connected with most of the developed markets, while less connected with commodity markets. It is evident that bonds offer limited diversification benefits for the developed markets. We also document a heightening of the spillover effect during periods of crisis. Our results are consistent with several robustness tests.<sup>8</sup> The time-frequency analysis shows that the stock market of France is the leading equity market in the short run, while gold leads in the long run. The time-frequency analysis shows that diversification benefits with gold can be more advantageous if it is intended for the long term. The contagion, as shown by the increase in correlation, is evident in the high-frequency scale during a period of crisis. Finally, we document that investors can use long-short (buy-sell) portfolio strategies based on shock-transmitter and shock-receiver asset classifications for portfolio risk management. Specifically, we note that a portfolio strategy that is long on shock-receivers and short on shock-transmitters generates higher hedging effectiveness with lower costs. This is an interesting and important conclusion for efficient portfolio management strategies.<p>Our study extends the related literature on spillover and connectedness primarily in three ways. First, our study provides portfolio-based diversification implications for the spillover and connectedness information. A strategic goal for investors and portfolio managers is to identify new investment strategies to maximize risk-adjusted gains through portfolio diversification. Our findings, in terms of long-short portfolio analysis using the magnitude and direction of the net spillover, can have beneficial implications to investment managers in an uncertain market environment. Second, our study extends the spillover and connectedness literature with specific focus on a large data set, spanning over the post-financial crisis period. Apart from the limited available research on this issue in the post-crisis period, there is also inadequate evidence on the use of a wide range of commodity, bond, and VIX financial markets, along with equities. Despite a large and growing body of spillover literature and increasing attention to the financialization of the commodity market (Ciner et al. 2018), the existing literature is limited about the direction of spillovers across commodity markets. In this regard, our findings can offer better policy and decision measures for regulators and investors. Third, this study also investigates the market integration and contagion using a time-frequency approach. The analysis of wavelet multiple cross-correlations helps detect the markets or assets that play a leading role in market integration, and wavelet local multiple correlations provide the opportunity to identify the contagion effects during a crisis or volatile period.<p>The rest of the paper is organized as follows.
